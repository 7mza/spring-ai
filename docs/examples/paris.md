**Paris**
`{{efn|{{IPA|en|ˈpærɪs|lang}}, {{respell|PARR|iss}};<ref>{{cite web |title=Paris |url=https://www.ldoceonline.com/dictionary/paris |website=Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English Online |access-date=31 October 2025}}</ref> {{IPA|fr|paʁi|lang|audio=Paris Pronunciation in French by James Tamim.ogg}}}}`
{=mediawiki}
is the [capital](Capital_city "wikilink") and [largest
city](List_of_communes_in_France_with_over_20,000_inhabitants "wikilink")
of [France](France "wikilink"), with an estimated city population of
2.04 million in an area of
`{{convert|105.4|km2|sqmi|abbr=on}}`{=mediawiki}, and a metropolitan
population of 13.2 million as of
`{{as of|bare=y|2026|January}}`{=mediawiki}.[^1] Located on the river
[Seine](Seine "wikilink") in the centre of the
[Île-de-France](Île-de-France "wikilink") region, it is the [largest
metropolitan area](List_of_metropolitan_areas_in_Europe "wikilink") and
[fourth-most populous
city](List_of_cities_in_the_European_Union_by_population_within_city_limits "wikilink")
in the [European Union](European_Union "wikilink") (EU). Nicknamed the
\"City of Light\", partly because of its role in the [Age of
Enlightenment](Age_of_Enlightenment "wikilink"), Paris has been one of
the world\'s major centres of [finance](finance "wikilink"),
[diplomacy](diplomacy "wikilink"), [commerce](commerce "wikilink"),
[culture](culture "wikilink"), [fashion](Fashion_capital "wikilink"),
and [gastronomy](gastronomy "wikilink") since the 17th century [^2]

Administratively, Paris is divided into [twenty
arrondissements](Arrondissements_of_Paris "wikilink") (districts), each
having their own cultural identity.
[Haussmann](Georges-Eugène_Haussmann "wikilink")\'s
[renovation](Haussmann's_renovation_of_Paris "wikilink") of Paris, which
created new boulevards, parks, and public works, gave birth to a modern
city known as the \"[capital of the 19th
century](Paris,_Capital_of_the_19th_Century "wikilink")\". Paris is a
major railway, motorway, and air-transport hub; in 2024 [Charles de
Gaulle Airport](Charles_de_Gaulle_Airport "wikilink") was the EU\'s
[busiest airport](List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_Europe "wikilink").
Paris has one of the most [sustainable
transportation](sustainable_transportation "wikilink") systems in the
world and is one of only two cities that have received the [Sustainable
Transport Award](Sustainable_Transport_Award "wikilink") twice.[^3] Its
[Art Nouveau](Art_Nouveau "wikilink")-decorated
[Métro](Paris_Métro "wikilink") has become a symbol of the city. Paris
is known for its museums and architectural landmarks: the [Musée
d\'Orsay](Musée_d'Orsay "wikilink"), [Musée Marmottan
Monet](Musée_Marmottan_Monet "wikilink"), and [Musée de
l\'Orangerie](Musée_de_l'Orangerie "wikilink") are noted for their
collections of French [Impressionist](Impressionism "wikilink") art,
while the
`{{lang|fr|[[Musée National d'Art Moderne]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki},
[Musée Rodin](Musée_Rodin "wikilink"), and [Musée
Picasso](Musée_Picasso "wikilink") are noted for their collections of
[modern](Modern_art "wikilink") and [contemporary
art](contemporary_art "wikilink"). Parts of the city along the
[Seine](Seine "wikilink") have been designated as a
[UNESCO](UNESCO "wikilink") [World Heritage
Site](World_Heritage_Site "wikilink")
`{{as of|since=y|1991|lc=y}}`{=mediawiki}.

The [president of France](president_of_France "wikilink") and both
houses of the [French Parliament](French_Parliament "wikilink") sit in
Paris. Paris is home to several [United
Nations](United_Nations "wikilink") organisations, including
[UNESCO](UNESCO "wikilink"), as well as other international
organisations such as the [OECD](OECD "wikilink"), the [International
Bureau of Weights and
Measures](International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures "wikilink") (in
neighbouring [Saint-Cloud](Saint-Cloud "wikilink")), the [International
Energy Agency](International_Energy_Agency "wikilink"), the
[Organisation internationale de la
Francophonie](Organisation_internationale_de_la_Francophonie "wikilink"),
the [International Federation for Human
Rights](International_Federation_for_Human_Rights "wikilink"), and the
[Fédération internationale de
l\'Automobile](Fédération_Internationale_de_l'Automobile "wikilink"),
along with European bodies such as the [European Space
Agency](European_Space_Agency "wikilink"), the [European Banking
Authority](European_Banking_Authority "wikilink"), and the [European
Securities and Markets
Authority](European_Securities_and_Markets_Authority "wikilink"). The
city [hosts many sporting events](Sport_in_Paris "wikilink"), such as
the [French Open](French_Open "wikilink"), and is the home of the
[association football](association_football "wikilink") club [Paris
Saint-Germain](Paris_Saint-Germain_FC "wikilink") and the [rugby
union](rugby_union "wikilink") club [Stade
Français](Stade_Français "wikilink"). Paris has also hosted the [Summer
Olympics](Summer_Olympics "wikilink") three times.

## Etymology

The ancient [oppidum](oppidum "wikilink") that corresponds to the modern
city of Paris was first mentioned in the mid-1st century BC by [Julius
Caesar](Julius_Caesar "wikilink") as _Lutetia Parisiorum_
(\'[Lutetia](Lutetia "wikilink") of the
[Parisii](<Parisii_(France)> "wikilink")\') and is later attested as
_Parision_ in the 5th century AD, then as _Paris_ in 1265.`{{Sfn|Nègre|1990|p=155}}`{=mediawiki}[^4] During the Roman period,
it was commonly known as `{{Lang|la|Lutetia}}`{=mediawiki} in Latin,
which is interpreted as either stemming from the
[Celtic](Celtic_languages "wikilink") root _\*lukot-_ (\'mouse\'), or
from \*_luto-_ (\'marsh,
swamp\').`{{Sfn|Lambert|1994|p=38}}`{=mediawiki}`{{Sfn|Delamarre|2003|p=211}}`{=mediawiki}[^5]

The name _Paris_ is derived from its early inhabitants, the
[Parisii](<Parisii_(France)> "wikilink"), a [Gallic](Gauls "wikilink")
tribe from the [Iron Age](La_Tène_culture "wikilink") and the [Roman
period](Roman_period "wikilink").`{{Sfn|Delamarre|2003|p=247}}`{=mediawiki}
The meaning of the Gaulish [ethnonym](ethnonym "wikilink") remains
debated. According to [Xavier Delamarre](Xavier_Delamarre "wikilink"),
it may derive from the Celtic root _pario-_
(\'cauldron\').`{{Sfn|Delamarre|2003|p=247}}`{=mediawiki} [Alfred
Holder](Alfred_Holder "wikilink") interpreted the name as \'the makers\'
or \'the commanders\', by comparing it to the
[Welsh](Welsh_language "wikilink") _peryff_ (\'lord, commander\'), both
possibly descending from a [Proto-Celtic](Proto-Celtic "wikilink") form
reconstructed as \*_kwar-is-io_-.`{{Sfn|Busse|2006|p=199}}`{=mediawiki}
Alternatively, [Pierre-Yves Lambert](Pierre-Yves_Lambert "wikilink")
proposed to translate _Parisii_ as the \'spear people\', by connecting
the first element to the [Old Irish](Old_Irish "wikilink") _carr_
(\'spear\'), derived from an earlier \*_kwar-sā_.[^6]

Residents of the city are known in English as Parisians and in French as
_Parisiens_ (`{{IPA|fr|paʁizjɛ̃||Parisien2.ogg}}`{=mediawiki}). They are
also pejoratively called _Parigots_
(`{{IPA|fr|paʁiɡo||Parigot.ogg}}`{=mediawiki}).`{{sfn|Dottin|2018|p=535}}`{=mediawiki}

### Origins

```{=mediawiki}
{{main|Lutetia}}
```

![Gold coins minted by the [Parisii](Parisii_(Gaul) "wikilink"), 1st
century
BC](ParisiiCoins.jpg "Gold coins minted by the Parisii, 1st century BC")
The _[Parisii](<Parisii_(Gaul)> "wikilink")\_ people inhabited the Paris
area from around the middle of the 3rd century
BC.`{{sfn|Cunliffe|2004|p=201}}`{=mediawiki} One of the area\'s major
north--south trade routes crossed the [Seine](Seine "wikilink") on the
[Île de la Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink"), which gradually became an
important trading
centre.`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=25}}`{=mediawiki} The Parisii
traded with many river towns (some as far away as the Iberian Peninsula)
and minted their own coins.`{{sfn|Schmidt|2009|pp=65–70}}`{=mediawiki}

[Julius Caesar](Julius_Caesar "wikilink")
[conquered](Gallic_Wars "wikilink") the [Paris
Basin](Paris_Basin "wikilink") for the [Roman
Republic](Roman_Republic "wikilink") in 52 BC and began the Roman
settlement on Paris\'s [Left
Bank](Rive_Gauche "wikilink").`{{sfn|Schmidt|2009|pp=88–104}}`{=mediawiki}
The Roman town was originally called [Lutetia](Lutetia "wikilink") (more
fully, _Lutetia Parisiorum_, \"Lutetia of the Parisii\", modern French
_Lutèce_). It became a prosperous city with a forum, baths, temples,
theatres, and an
[amphitheatre](amphitheatre "wikilink").`{{sfn|Schmidt|2009|pp=154–167}}`{=mediawiki}

By the end of the [Western Roman
Empire](Western_Roman_Empire "wikilink"), the town was known as
_Parisius_, a [Latin](Latin_language "wikilink") name that would later
become _Paris_ in French.`{{Sfn|Meunier|2014|p=12}}`{=mediawiki}
[Christianity](Christianity "wikilink") was introduced in the middle of
the 3rd century AD by Saint [Denis](Saint_Denis_of_Paris "wikilink"),
the first Bishop of Paris: according to legend, when he refused to
renounce his faith before the Roman occupiers, he was beheaded on the
hill which became known as _Mons Martyrum_ (Latin \"Hill of Martyrs\"),
later \"[Montmartre](Montmartre "wikilink")\", from where he walked
headless to the north of the city; the place where he fell and was
buried became an important religious shrine, the [Basilica of
Saint-Denis](Basilica_of_Saint-Denis "wikilink"), and many French kings
are buried there.`{{sfn|Schmidt|2009|pp=210–211}}`{=mediawiki}

![The [Wall of Philippe Auguste](Wall_of_Philip_II_Augustus "wikilink"),
oldest city wall in Paris
](FranceEnglandCard2_052.jpg "The Wall of Philippe Auguste, oldest city wall in Paris ")
[Clovis the Frank](Clovis_I "wikilink"), the first king of the
[Merovingian dynasty](Merovingian_dynasty "wikilink"), made the city his
capital from 508.[^7] As the Frankish domination of Gaul began, there
was a gradual immigration by the [Franks](Franks "wikilink") to Paris,
and the Parisian [Francien](Francien_language "wikilink") dialects were
born. Fortification of the [Île de la Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink")
failed to avert [sacking by Vikings in
845](<Siege_of_Paris_(845)> "wikilink"). Still, Paris\'s strategic
importance---with its bridges preventing ships from passing---was
established by successful defence in the [Siege of Paris
(885--886)](<Siege_of_Paris_(885–886)> "wikilink"), for which the then
[Count of Paris](Count_of_Paris "wikilink") (_comte de Paris_), [Odo of
France](Odo_of_France "wikilink"), was elected king of [West
Francia](West_Francia "wikilink").`{{sfn|Jones|1994|p=48}}`{=mediawiki}
From the [Capetian](House_of_Capet "wikilink") dynasty that began with
the 987 election of [Hugh Capet](Hugh_Capet "wikilink"), Count of Paris
and [Duke of the Franks](Duke_of_the_Franks "wikilink") (_duc des
Francs_), as king of a unified West Francia, Paris gradually became the
largest and most prosperous city in
France.`{{sfn|Schmidt|2009|pp=210–211}}`{=mediawiki}

### High and Late Middle Ages to Louis XIV {#high_and_late_middle_ages_to_louis_xiv}

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Paris in the Middle Ages|Paris in the 16th century|Paris in the 17th century}}
```

![The [Palais de la Cité](Palais_de_la_Cité "wikilink") and
[Sainte-Chapelle](Sainte-Chapelle "wikilink"), viewed from the Left
Bank, from the [Très Riches Heures du duc de
Berry](Très_Riches_Heures_du_duc_de_Berry "wikilink") (month of June)
(1410)](Palais_de_la_Cite.jpg "The Palais de la Cité and Sainte-Chapelle, viewed from the Left Bank, from the Très Riches Heures du duc de Berry (month of June) (1410)")

By the end of the 12th century, Paris had become the political,
economic, religious, and cultural capital of
France.`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=27}}`{=mediawiki} [Maurice de
Sully](Maurice_de_Sully "wikilink"), bishop of Paris, started the
construction of the [Notre Dame
Cathedral](Notre_Dame_de_Paris "wikilink") in 1163, and was completed
after 182 years.[^8] After the marshland between the river Seine and
`{{clarify|text=its slower 'dead arm' to its north was filled in|date=August 2025}}`{=mediawiki}
from around the 10th century,`{{sfn|Bussmann|1985|p=22}}`{=mediawiki}
Paris\'s cultural centre began to move to the Right Bank. In 1137, a new
city marketplace (today\'s [Les Halles](Les_Halles "wikilink")) replaced
the two smaller ones on the Île de la Cité and [Place de Grève (Place de
l\'Hôtel de
Ville)](Place_de_l'Hôtel-de-Ville_–_Esplanade_de_la_Libération "wikilink").`{{sfn|de Vitriaco|Hinnebusch|1972|p=262}}`
{=mediawiki}
The latter location housed the headquarters of Paris\'s river trade
corporation. This organisation later became, unofficially (although
formally in later years), Paris\'s first municipal government.

In the late 12th century, [Philip
Augustus](Philip_II_of_France "wikilink") extended the
[Louvre](Louvre "wikilink") fortress to defend the city against river
invasions from the west, gave the city its first walls between 1190 and
1215, rebuilt its bridges to either side of its central island, and
paved its main thoroughfares.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|pp=36–40}}`{=mediawiki}
In 1190, he transformed Paris\'s former cathedral school into a
student-teacher corporation that would become the [University of
Paris](University_of_Paris "wikilink") and would draw students from all
of
Europe.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|pp=28–29}}`{=mediawiki}`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=27}}`{=mediawiki}

With 200,000 inhabitants in 1328, Paris, then already the capital of
France, was the most populous city of Europe. By comparison, London in
1300 had 80,000 inhabitants.[^9] Around 1340 the city had between
250,000 and 300,000 inhabitants.[^10] By the early fourteenth century,
so much filth had collected inside urban Europe that French and Italian
cities were naming streets after human waste. In medieval Paris, several
street names were inspired by `{{Lang|fr|merde}}`{=mediawiki}, the
French word for \"shit\".[^11] ![The [Hôtel de
Sens](Hôtel_de_Sens "wikilink") (`{{Circa}}`{=mediawiki} 15th--16th),
former residence of the Archbishop of
Sens](P1030887_Paris_IV_hôtel_de_Sens_rwk.JPG "The Hôtel de Sens ( 15th–16th), former residence of the Archbishop of Sens")
During the [Hundred Years\' War](Hundred_Years'_War "wikilink"), Paris
was occupied by England-friendly [Burgundian
forces](Burgundian_State "wikilink") from
[1418](Paris_coup_d'état_and_massacres_of_1418 "wikilink"), before being
occupied outright by the English when [Henry V of
England](Henry_V_of_England "wikilink") entered the French capital in
1420;[^12] despite a 1429 effort by [Joan of
Arc](Joan_of_Arc "wikilink") to liberate the
city,`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|pages=52–53}}`{=mediawiki} it would remain under
English occupation until 1436.

In the late 16th century [French Wars of
Religion](French_Wars_of_Religion "wikilink"), Paris was a stronghold of
the [Catholic League](<Catholic_League_(French)> "wikilink"), the
organisers of 24 August 1572 [St. Bartholomew\'s Day
massacre](St._Bartholomew's_Day_massacre "wikilink") in which thousands
of French Protestants were
killed.[^13]`{{sfn|Bayrou|1994|pp=121–130}}`{=mediawiki} The conflicts
ended when pretender to the throne [Henry
IV](Henry_IV_of_France "wikilink"), after converting to Catholicism to
gain entry to the capital, entered the city in 1594 to claim the crown
of France. This king made several improvements to the capital during his
reign: he completed the construction of Paris\'s first uncovered,
sidewalk-lined bridge, the [Pont Neuf](Pont_Neuf "wikilink"), built a
Louvre extension connecting it to the [Tuileries
Palace](Tuileries_Palace "wikilink"), and created the first Paris
residential square, the Place Royale, now [Place des
Vosges](Place_des_Vosges "wikilink"). Despite Henry IV\'s efforts to
improve city circulation, the narrowness of Paris\'s streets was a
contributing factor in his assassination near [Les
Halles](Les_Halles "wikilink") marketplace in 1610.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=577}}`{=mediawiki}

During the 17th century, [Cardinal
Richelieu](Cardinal_Richelieu "wikilink"), chief minister of [Louis
XIII](Louis_XIII "wikilink"), was determined to make Paris the most
beautiful city in Europe. He built five new bridges, a new chapel for
the [College of Sorbonne](College_of_Sorbonne "wikilink"), and a palace
for himself, the [Palais-Cardinal](Palais-Royal "wikilink"). After
Richelieu died in 1642, it was renamed the
[Palais-Royal](Palais-Royal "wikilink").`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=582}}`{=mediawiki}

![Lutetia Parisiorum vulgo Paris, Plan de Paris en 1657, [Jan
Janssonius](Jan_Janssonius "wikilink")](Plan_de_Paris_en_1657.JPG "Lutetia Parisiorum vulgo Paris, Plan de Paris en 1657, Jan Janssonius")
Due to the Parisian uprisings during the [Fronde](Fronde "wikilink")
civil war, [Louis XIV](Louis_XIV "wikilink") moved his court to a new
palace, [Versailles](Palace_of_Versailles "wikilink"), in 1682. Although
no longer the capital of France, arts and sciences in the city
flourished with the [Comédie-Française](Comédie-Française "wikilink"),
the Academy of Painting, and the [French Academy of
Sciences](French_Academy_of_Sciences "wikilink"). To demonstrate that
the city was safe from attack, the king had the [city
walls](City_walls_of_Paris "wikilink") demolished and replaced with
tree-lined boulevards that would become the _[Grands
Boulevards](Boulevards_of_Paris#The_grands_boulevards "wikilink")_.`{{Sfn|Combeau|2003|pp=42–43}}`{=mediawiki}
Other marks of his reign were the [Collège des
Quatre-Nations](Collège_des_Quatre-Nations "wikilink"), the [Place
Vendôme](Place_Vendôme "wikilink"), the [Place des
Victoires](Place_des_Victoires "wikilink"), and [Les
Invalides](Les_Invalides "wikilink").`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|pp=590–591}}`{=mediawiki}

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Paris in the 18th century|Paris during the Second Empire|Haussmann's renovation of Paris}}
```

Paris grew in population from about 400,000 in 1640 to 650,000 in 1780.[^14] A new boulevard named the
[Champs-Élysées](Champs-Élysées "wikilink") extended the city west to
[Étoile](Place_Charles_de_Gaulle "wikilink"),`{{Sfn|Combeau|2003|pp=45–47}}`{=mediawiki}
while the working-class neighbourhood of the [Faubourg
Saint-Antoine](Faubourg_Saint-Antoine "wikilink") on the eastern side of
the city grew increasingly crowded with poor migrant workers from other
regions of France.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|pp=129–133}}`{=mediawiki} Paris\'s
role in [education](Education_in_France "wikilink") and
[sciences](Science_and_technology_in_France "wikilink") led Paris to be
known as the City of Light during the [age of
Enlightenment](age_of_Enlightenment "wikilink") in the 18th
century.[^15]`{{efn|{{langx|fr|la Ville Lumière}}}}`{=mediawiki}

![The storming of the [Bastille](Bastille "wikilink") on 14 July 1789,
by [Jean-Pierre
Houël](Jean-Pierre_Houël "wikilink")](Prise_de_la_Bastille.jpg "The storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789, by Jean-Pierre Houël")
In the summer of 1789, Paris became the centre stage of the [French
Revolution](French_Revolution "wikilink"). On 14 July, a mob seized the
arsenal at the [Invalides](Les_Invalides "wikilink"), acquiring
thousands of guns, with which it [stormed the
Bastille](Storming_of_the_Bastille "wikilink"), a principal symbol of
royal authority. The first independent [Paris
Commune](<Paris_Commune_(1789–1795)> "wikilink"), or city council, met in
the _Hôtel de Ville_ and elected a [Mayor](<Mayor_(France)> "wikilink"),
the astronomer [Jean Sylvain Bailly](Jean_Sylvain_Bailly "wikilink"), on
15 July.`{{sfn|Paine|1998|p=453}}`{=mediawiki}

[Louis XVI](Louis_XVI "wikilink") and the royal family were [brought to
Paris](Women's_march_on_Versailles "wikilink") and incarcerated in the
Tuileries Palace. In 1793, as the revolution turned increasingly
radical, the king, queen, and mayor were beheaded by
[guillotine](guillotine "wikilink") in the [Reign of
Terror](Reign_of_Terror "wikilink"), along with more than 16,000 others
throughout France.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=674}}`{=mediawiki} The property
of the aristocracy and the church was
[nationalised](Biens_nationaux "wikilink"), and the city\'s churches
were closed, sold, or
demolished.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=144}}`{=mediawiki} A succession of
revolutionary factions ruled Paris until [9 November
1799](Coup_of_18_Brumaire "wikilink") (_coup d\'état du 18 brumaire_),
when [Napoleon Bonaparte](Napoleon_Bonaparte "wikilink") seized power as
First Consul.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=147}}`{=mediawiki}

The population of Paris had dropped by 100,000 during the Revolution,
but after 1799 it surged with 160,000 new residents, reaching 660,000 by 1815.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=148}}`{=mediawiki} Napoleon replaced the
elected government of Paris with a prefect who reported directly to him.
He began erecting monuments to military glory, including the [Arc de
Triomphe](Arc_de_Triomphe "wikilink"), and improved the neglected
infrastructure of the city with new fountains, the [Canal de
l\'Ourcq](Canal_de_l'Ourcq "wikilink"), [Père Lachaise
Cemetery](Père_Lachaise_Cemetery "wikilink") and the city\'s first metal
bridge, the [Pont des Arts](Pont_des_Arts "wikilink"). The [Arc de
Triomphe](Arc_de_Triomphe "wikilink") was eventually completed in 1836.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=148}}`{=mediawiki}

![The [Eiffel Tower](Eiffel_Tower "wikilink"), under construction in
November 1888, startled Parisians---and the world---with its
modernity.](Louis-Emile_Durandelle,_The_Eiffel_Tower_-_State_of_the_Construction,_1888.jpg "The Eiffel Tower, under construction in November 1888, startled Parisians—and the world—with its modernity.")
During the [Restoration](Bourbon_Restoration_in_France "wikilink"), the
bridges and squares of Paris were returned to their pre-Revolution
names; the [July Revolution](July_Revolution "wikilink") in 1830
(commemorated by the [July Column](July_Column "wikilink") on the [Place
de la Bastille](Place_de_la_Bastille "wikilink")) brought to power a
constitutional monarch, [Louis Philippe I](Louis_Philippe_I "wikilink").
The first railway line to Paris opened in 1837, beginning a new period
of massive migration from the
[provinces](Provinces_of_France "wikilink") to the
city.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=148}}`{=mediawiki} In 1848, Louis-Philippe
was overthrown by a [popular
uprising](French_Revolution_of_1848 "wikilink") in the streets of Paris.
His successor, [Napoleon III](Napoleon_III "wikilink"), alongside the
newly appointed prefect of the Seine, [Georges-Eugène
Haussmann](Georges-Eugène_Haussmann "wikilink"), launched a huge public
works project to build wide new boulevards, a new opera house, a central
market, new aqueducts, sewers and parks, including the [Bois de
Boulogne](Bois_de_Boulogne "wikilink") and [Bois de
Vincennes](Bois_de_Vincennes "wikilink").`{{sfn|De Moncan|2012|pp=7–35}}`{=mediawiki}
In 1860, Napoleon III annexed the surrounding towns and created eight
new arrondissements, expanding Paris to its current
limits.`{{sfn|De Moncan|2012|pp=7–35}}`{=mediawiki}

During the [Franco-Prussian War](Franco-Prussian_War "wikilink")
(1870--1871), Paris was besieged by the [Prussian
Army](Prussian_Army "wikilink"). Following several months of blockade,
hunger, and then bombardment by the Prussians, the city was forced to
surrender on 28 January 1871. After seizing power in Paris on 28 March,
a revolutionary government known as the [Paris
Commune](Paris_Commune "wikilink") held power for two months, before
being harshly suppressed by the French army during the \"[Bloody
Week](Semaine_sanglante "wikilink")\" at the end of May 1871.`{{sfn|Rougerie|2014|p=118}}`{=mediawiki}

In the late 19th century, Paris hosted two major international
expositions: the [1889 Universal
Exposition](<Exposition_Universelle_(1889)> "wikilink"), which featured
the new Eiffel Tower, was held to mark the centennial of the French
Revolution; and the [1900 Universal
Exposition](<Exposition_Universelle_(1900)> "wikilink") gave Paris the
[Pont Alexandre III](Pont_Alexandre_III "wikilink"), the [Grand
Palais](Grand_Palais "wikilink"), the
`{{Lang|fr|[[Petit Palais]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki} and the first
[Paris Métro](Paris_Métro "wikilink")
line.`{{sfn|Fraser|Spalding|2011|p=117}}`{=mediawiki} Paris became the
laboratory of [Naturalism](<naturalism_(literature)> "wikilink") ([Émile
Zola](Émile_Zola "wikilink")) and
[Symbolism](<Symbolism_(arts)> "wikilink") ([Charles
Baudelaire](Charles_Baudelaire "wikilink") and [Paul
Verlaine](Paul_Verlaine "wikilink")), and of
[Impressionism](Impressionism "wikilink") in art
([Courbet](Courbet "wikilink"), [Manet](Manet "wikilink"),
[Monet](Claude_Monet "wikilink"),
[Renoir](Renoir "wikilink")).`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|pp=490–491}}`{=mediawiki}

### 20th and 21st centuries {#th_and_21st_centuries}

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Paris in the Belle Époque|Paris during the First World War|Paris between the Wars (1919–1939)|Paris in World War II|History of Paris (1946–2000)}}
```

By 1901, the population of Paris had grown to about
2,715,000.`{{sfn|Combeau|2003|p=61}}`{=mediawiki} At the beginning of
the century, artists from around the world, including [Pablo
Picasso](Pablo_Picasso "wikilink"),
[Modigliani](Amedeo_Modigliani "wikilink"), and [Henri
Matisse](Henri_Matisse "wikilink") made Paris their home. It was the
birthplace of [Fauvism](Fauvism "wikilink"), [Cubism](Cubism "wikilink")
and [abstract
art](abstract_art "wikilink"),`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=497}}`{=mediawiki}[^16]
and authors such as [Marcel Proust](Marcel_Proust "wikilink") were
exploring new approaches to
literature.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=491}}`{=mediawiki}

During the [First World War](First_World_War "wikilink"), Paris
sometimes found itself on the front line; 600 to 1,000 Paris taxis
played a small but highly important symbolic role in transporting 6,000
soldiers to the front line at the [First Battle of the
Marne](First_Battle_of_the_Marne "wikilink"). The city was also bombed
by [Zeppelins](Zeppelin "wikilink") and shelled by German [long-range
guns](Paris_Gun "wikilink").`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=750}}`{=mediawiki} In
the years after the war, known as _[Les Années
Folles](<Paris_between_the_Wars_(1919–1939)> "wikilink")\_, Paris continued
to be a mecca for writers, musicians and artists from around the world,
including [Ernest Hemingway](Ernest_Hemingway "wikilink"), [Igor
Stravinsky](Igor_Stravinsky "wikilink"), [James
Joyce](James_Joyce "wikilink"), [Josephine
Baker](Josephine_Baker "wikilink"), [Eva
Kotchever](Eva_Kotchever "wikilink"), [Henry
Miller](Henry_Miller "wikilink"), [Anaïs Nin](Anaïs_Nin "wikilink"),
[Sidney
Bechet](Sidney_Bechet "wikilink")`{{sfn|Shack|2001|}}`{=mediawiki} and
[Salvador Dalí](Salvador_Dalí "wikilink").[^17]

In the years after the [peace
conference](Paris_Peace_Conference,_1919 "wikilink"), the city was also
home to growing numbers of students and activists from [French
colonies](French_colonial_empire "wikilink") and other Asian and African
countries, who later became leaders of their countries, such as [Ho Chi
Minh](Ho_Chi_Minh "wikilink"), [Zhou Enlai](Zhou_Enlai "wikilink") and
[Léopold Sédar Senghor](Léopold_Sédar_Senghor "wikilink").[^18]

![General [Charles de Gaulle](Charles_de_Gaulle "wikilink") on the
Champs-Élysées celebrating the liberation of Paris, 26 August
1944](The_Liberation_of_Paris,_25_-_26_August_1944_HU66477.jpg "General Charles de Gaulle on the Champs-Élysées celebrating the liberation of Paris, 26 August 1944")
On 14 June 1940, the German army marched into Paris, which had been
declared an \"[open
city](open_city "wikilink")\".`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=217}}`{=mediawiki}
On 16--17 July 1942, following German orders, the French police and
gendarmes arrested 12,884 Jews, including 4,115 children, and confined
them during five days at the [_Vel
d\'Hiv_](Vel'_d'Hiv_Roundup "wikilink"), from which they were
transported by train to the extermination camp at
[Auschwitz](Auschwitz_concentration_camp "wikilink"). None of the
children came
back.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=637}}`{=mediawiki}`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=218}}`{=mediawiki}
On 25 August 1944, the city was liberated by the [French 2nd Armoured
Division](<2nd_Armored_Division_(France)> "wikilink") and the [4th
Infantry Division](<4th_Infantry_Division_(United_States)> "wikilink") of
the [United States Army](United_States_Army "wikilink"). General
[Charles de Gaulle](Charles_de_Gaulle "wikilink") led a huge and
emotional crowd down the Champs Élysées towards Notre Dame de Paris and
made a rousing speech from the [Hôtel de
Ville](Hôtel_de_Ville,_Paris "wikilink").`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|pp=242–243}}`{=mediawiki}

Paris became involved in the [Algerian War](Algerian_War "wikilink") for
independence, which occurred in the 1950s and 1960s; in August 1961, the
pro-independence [FLN](<National_Liberation_Front_(Algeria)> "wikilink"),
targeted and killed 11 Paris policemen, leading to the imposition of a
curfew on Muslims of Algeria (who, at that time, were French citizens).
On 17 October 1961, an unauthorised but peaceful protest demonstration
of Algerians against the curfew led to [violent
confrontations](1961_Paris_massacre "wikilink") between the police and
demonstrators, in which at least 40 people were killed. The
anti-independence [Organisation armée
secrète](Organisation_armée_secrète "wikilink") (OAS) carried out a
series of bombings in Paris throughout 1961 and 1962.[^19]`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=658}}`{=mediawiki}

In May 1968, protesting students occupied the
[Sorbonne](<Sorbonne_(building)> "wikilink") and put up barricades in the
[Latin Quarter](Latin_Quarter,_Paris "wikilink"). Thousands of Parisian
blue-collar workers joined the students, and the movement grew into a
two-week general strike. Supporters of the government won the June
elections by a large majority. The [May 1968 events in
France](May_1968_events_in_France "wikilink") resulted in the break-up
of the University of Paris into 13 independent
campuses.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=226}}`{=mediawiki} In 1975, the National
Assembly changed the status of Paris to that of other French cities and,
on 25 March 1977, [Jacques Chirac](Jacques_Chirac "wikilink") became the
first elected mayor of Paris since 1793.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=260}}`{=mediawiki} The [Tour
Maine-Montparnasse](Tour_Montparnasse "wikilink"), the tallest building
in the city at 57 storeys and `{{cvt|210|m|ft|0|abbr=off}}`{=mediawiki}
high, was built between 1969 and 1973. It was highly controversial, and
it remains the only building in the centre of the city over 32 storeys
high.`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|p=222}}`{=mediawiki} The population of Paris
dropped from 2,850,000 in 1954 to 2,152,000 in 1990, as middle-class
families moved to the
suburbs.`{{sfn|Combeau|2003|pp=107–108}}`{=mediawiki} A suburban railway
network, the [RER](Réseau_Express_Régional "wikilink") (Réseau Express
Régional), was built to complement the Métro; the
[Périphérique](<Périphérique_(Paris)> "wikilink") expressway encircling
the city was completed in 1973.`{{sfn|Bell|de-Shalit|2011|p=247}}`{=mediawiki} The bank of the
[Seine](Seine "wikilink") in the city centre, which includes the
[Louvre](Louvre "wikilink") and the Eiffel Tower was classified as a
[UNESCO World Heritage Site](UNESCO_World_Heritage_Site "wikilink") in 1991.[^20]

Most of the postwar presidents of the [Fifth
Republic](French_Fifth_Republic "wikilink") wanted to leave their own
monuments in Paris; President [Georges
Pompidou](Georges_Pompidou "wikilink") started the [Centre Georges
Pompidou](Centre_Georges_Pompidou "wikilink") (1977), [Valéry Giscard
d\'Estaing](Valéry_Giscard_d'Estaing "wikilink") began the [Musée
d\'Orsay](Musée_d'Orsay "wikilink") (1986); President [François
Mitterrand](François_Mitterrand "wikilink") had the [Opéra
Bastille](Opéra_Bastille "wikilink") built (1985--1989), the new site of
the _[Bibliothèque nationale de
France](Bibliothèque_nationale_de_France "wikilink")_ (1996), the [Arche
de la Défense](Arche_de_la_Défense "wikilink") (1985--1989) in [La
Défense](La_Défense "wikilink"), as well as the [Louvre
Pyramid](Louvre_Pyramid "wikilink") with its underground courtyard
(1983--1989); [Jacques Chirac](Jacques_Chirac "wikilink") (2006), the
[Musée du quai
Branly](Musée_du_Quai_Branly_–_Jacques_Chirac "wikilink").`{{sfn|Sarmant|2012|pp=226–230}}`{=mediawiki}

In the early 21st century, the population of Paris began to increase
slowly again, as more young people moved into the city. It reached 2.25
million in 2011. In March 2001, [Bertrand
Delanoë](Bertrand_Delanoë "wikilink") became the first socialist mayor;
he was re-elected in March 2008.[^21] Bertrand Delanoë also transformed
a section of the highway along the Left Bank of the Seine into an urban
promenade and park, the [Promenade des Berges de la
Seine](Promenade_des_Berges_de_la_Seine "wikilink"), which he
inaugurated in June 2013.[^22] ![Demonstrators at the [Place de la
République](Place_de_la_République "wikilink"), Paris, 11 January 2015,
during the [Republican marches](Republican_marches "wikilink") after the
[*Charlie Hebdo*
shooting](Charlie_Hebdo_shooting "wikilink")](<Marche_hommage_Charlie_hebdo_et_aux_victimes_des_attentats_de_janvier_2015_(17).jpg> "Demonstrators at the Place de la République, Paris, 11 January 2015, during the Republican marches after the Charlie Hebdo shooting")

In 2007, President [Nicolas Sarkozy](Nicolas_Sarkozy "wikilink")
launched the [Grand Paris](Grand_Paris "wikilink") project to integrate
Paris more closely with the towns in the region around it. After many
modifications, the new area, named the [Metropolis of Grand
Paris](Grand_Paris "wikilink"), with a population of 6.7 million, was
created on 1 January 2016.[^23] The City of Paris and the national
government approved the plans for the [Grand Paris
Express](Grand_Paris_Express "wikilink") four years later, totalling
`{{cvt|205|km|mi|abbr=off}}`{=mediawiki} of automated metro lines to
connect Paris, the innermost three departments around Paris, airports
and [high-speed rail (TGV)](TGV "wikilink") stations, at an estimated
cost of €35 billion.[^24] The system is scheduled to be completed by 2030.[^25]`{{update after|2030}}`{=mediawiki}

A [series of attacks](January_2015_Île-de-France_attacks "wikilink")
occurred in the Paris region, especially in Paris, in January 2015, the
most serious being the [shooting at the
offices](Charlie_Hebdo_shooting "wikilink") of [Charlie
Hebdo](Charlie_Hebdo "wikilink"). 17 people died in the attacks, which
[Al-Qaeda in the Arabian
Peninsula](Al-Qaeda_in_the_Arabian_Peninsula "wikilink") claimed
responsibility.[^26][^27] [1.5 million people marched in
Paris](Republican_marches "wikilink") in a show of solidarity against
terrorism and in support of freedom of speech.[^28] [Terrorist
attacks](November_2015_Paris_attacks "wikilink"), claimed by ISIL,[^29]
occurred again in November, which killed 130 people and injured more
than 350.[^30]

In an aim to limit the [effects of climate
change](effects_of_climate_change "wikilink") below 2 °C, the [Paris
Agreement](Paris_Agreement "wikilink") was signed by 196 nations of the
[United Nations Framework Convention on Climate
Change](United_Nations_Framework_Convention_on_Climate_Change "wikilink")
on 22 April 2016 in Paris.[^31] The following year, billionaire
[François Pinault](François_Pinault "wikilink") received a 50‑year lease
to convert the historic [Bourse de
Commerce](<Bourse_de_commerce_(Paris)> "wikilink") into a contemporary art
museum, overseen by [Japanese](Japanese_people "wikilink") architect
[Tadao Ando](Tadao_Ando "wikilink").[^32] Construction was delayed by
the [COVID-19 pandemic](COVID-19_pandemic "wikilink"), reopening in May 2021.[^33] The [Notre Dame Cathedral caught
fire](Notre-Dame_fire "wikilink") on 15 April 2019, beginning in the
eaves of its roof,[^34] engulfing the [spire](spire "wikilink") and most
of the roof. Firefighters were able to control the blaze, saving the
main structure including the [bell towers](bell_tower "wikilink") and
[rose windows](rose_window "wikilink"), and there were no injuries;[^35]
[it was reopened](Reopening_of_Notre-Dame_de_Paris "wikilink") on 7
December 2024.[^36]

In terms of international connectedness, as of 2024, Paris was one of
eight cities worldwide that was classified as an \"Alpha+\" city by the
[Globalization and World Cities Research
Network](Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network "wikilink").[^37]

## Geography

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Geography of Paris|Climate of Paris}}
```

![A 2022 satellite image of Paris, by ESA's
[Sentinel-2](Sentinel-2 "wikilink")
mission](Paris,_France_ESA24382977.jpeg "A 2022 satellite image of Paris, by ESA's Sentinel-2 mission")
Paris is located in northern central France, in a north-bending arc of
the river [Seine](Seine "wikilink"), whose crest includes two islands,
the [Île Saint-Louis](Île_Saint-Louis "wikilink") and the larger [Île de
la Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink"), which form the oldest part of
Paris. The river\'s mouth on the [English
Channel](English_Channel "wikilink") (_La Manche_) is about
`{{cvt|233|mi}}`{=mediawiki} downstream from Paris. Paris is spread
widely on both banks of the river.[^38] Overall, Paris is relatively
flat, and the lowest point is `{{cvt|35|m}}`{=mediawiki} [above sea
level](Above_mean_sea_level "wikilink"). Paris has several prominent
hills, the highest of which is [Montmartre](Montmartre "wikilink") at
`{{cvt|130|m|ft|0}}`{=mediawiki}.`{{sfn|Blackmore|McConnachie|2004|p=153}}`{=mediawiki}

Excluding the outlying parks of [Bois de
Boulogne](Bois_de_Boulogne "wikilink") and [Bois de
Vincennes](Bois_de_Vincennes "wikilink"), Paris covers an oval measuring
about `{{cvt|87|km2}}`{=mediawiki} in area, enclosed by the
`{{cvt|35|km|adj=on}}`{=mediawiki} ring road, the [Boulevard
Périphérique](Boulevard_Périphérique "wikilink").`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=69}}`{=mediawiki}
Paris\'s last major annexation of outlying territories in 1860 gave it
its modern form, and created the 20 clockwise-spiralling arrondissements
(municipal boroughs). From the 1860 area of
`{{cvt|78|km2}}`{=mediawiki}, the city limits were expanded marginally
to `{{cvt|86.9|km2}}`{=mediawiki} in the 1920s. In 1929, the Bois de
Boulogne and Bois de Vincennes forest parks were annexed to the city,
bringing its area to about `{{cvt|105|km2}}`{=mediawiki}.[^39] The
metropolitan area is `{{cvt|2300|km2}}`{=mediawiki}.[^40]

### Climate

![Autumn in `{{interlanguage link|Avenue Raphaël|fr}}`{=mediawiki},
Paris](<Paris_raining_autumn_cityscape_(8252181936).jpg> "Autumn in , Paris")

Paris has a [Köppen climate
classification](Köppen_climate_classification "wikilink") of Cfb, or an
[oceanic climate](oceanic_climate "wikilink"),[^41] typical of western
Europe.[^42] This climate type features cool winters and warm summers,
with average temperatures between `{{cvt|15|and|25|°C|°F}}`{=mediawiki}
in July.`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=309}}`{=mediawiki}[^43] Each
year there are a few days when the temperature rises above
`{{cvt|32|C}}`{=mediawiki}. Longer periods of more intense heat
sometimes occur, such as the [heat wave of
2003](2003_European_heat_wave "wikilink"), when temperatures exceeded
`{{cvt|30|°C}}`{=mediawiki} for weeks, reached
`{{cvt|40|°C}}`{=mediawiki} on some days, and rarely cooled down at
night.`{{sfn|Goldstein|2005|p=8}}`{=mediawiki}

The weather in [spring](<Spring_(season)> "wikilink") and
[autumn](autumn "wikilink") is generally mild during the day and cool at
night, but is unstable.[^44] In [winter](winter "wikilink"), sunshine is
scarce, days are cool, and nights are cold but generally above freezing,
with low temperatures around `{{cvt|3|°C}}`{=mediawiki}.[^45] Light
night frosts are quite common, but the temperature seldom dips below
`{{cvt|-5|°C}}`{=mediawiki}. Paris sometimes sees light snow or flurries
with or without accumulation.[^46] In [summer](summer "wikilink"), the
weather is warm, averaging `{{cvt|22|°C}}`{=mediawiki}, and there is 8
hours of sunshine on average per day.[^47]

Paris has an average annual precipitation of
`{{cvt|641|mm|in|1}}`{=mediawiki}, and experiences light rainfall
distributed evenly throughout the year.
`{{as of|2025|post=,}}`{=mediawiki} the highest recorded temperature was
`{{cvt|42.6|°C}}`{=mediawiki}, on 25 July 2019.[^48] The lowest was
`{{cvt|-23.9|°C}}`{=mediawiki}, on 10 December 1879.[^49]

![A map of the [arrondissements of
Paris](arrondissements_of_Paris "wikilink")](Paris_plan_jms.png "A map of the arrondissements of Paris")

For almost all of its long history, except for a few brief periods,
Paris was governed directly by representatives of the king, emperor, or
president of France. In 1974, Paris was granted municipal autonomy by
the National Assembly.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=334}}`{=mediawiki} The first
modern elected mayor of Paris was [Jacques
Chirac](Jacques_Chirac "wikilink"), elected in March 1977, becoming the
city\'s first mayor since 1871 and only the fourth since 1794. The
current mayor is [Emmanuel Grégoire](Emmanuel_Grégoire "wikilink"), a
member of the [Socialist Party](<Socialist_Party_(France)> "wikilink"),
who was elected in
[2026](2026_Paris_municipal_election "wikilink").[^50]

![The [Hôtel de Ville](Hôtel_de_Ville,_Paris "wikilink"), or city
hall](Paris,_Hôtel_de_Ville_--_2014_--_1714.jpg "The Hôtel de Ville, or city hall")

The mayor of Paris is [elected indirectly](indirect_election "wikilink")
by Paris voters. The voters of each of the city\'s 20 arrondissements
elect members to the _[Conseil de Paris](Conseil_de_Paris "wikilink")_
(Council of Paris), which elects the mayor. The council is composed of
163 members. Each arrondissement is allocated many seats, dependent upon
its population, from 10 members for each of the least-populated
arrondissements to 34 members for the most populated. The council is
elected using [closed list](closed_list "wikilink") [proportional
representation](proportional_representation "wikilink") in a [two-round
system](two-round_system "wikilink").[^51]

Party lists winning an absolute [majority](majority "wikilink") in the
first round -- or at least a [plurality](<plurality_(voting)> "wikilink")
in the second round -- automatically win half the seats of an
arrondissement. The remaining half of the seats are distributed
proportionally to all lists which win at least 5% of the vote, using the
[highest averages method](highest_averages_method "wikilink").[^52] This
ensures that the winning party or coalition always wins a majority of
the seats, even if they do not win an absolute majority of the
vote.[^53]

Before the [2020 Paris municipal
election](2020_Paris_municipal_election "wikilink"), each of Paris\'s 20
arrondissements had its own town hall and a directly elected council
(`{{Lang|fr|[[conseil d'arrondissement]]}}`{=mediawiki}), which elects
an arrondissement mayor.`{{sfn|Shales|2007|p=16}}`{=mediawiki} The
council of each arrondissement is composed of members of the Conseil de
Paris, and members who serve only on the council of the arrondissement.
The number of deputy mayors in each arrondissement varies depending on
its population. As of 1996, there were 20 arrondissement mayors and 120
deputy mayors.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=334}}`{=mediawiki} The creation of
[Paris Centre](Paris_Centre "wikilink"), a unified administrative
division with a single mayor covering the first four arrondissements,
took effect with the said 2020 election. The other 16 arrondissements
continue to have their own mayors.[^54]

### Métropole du Grand Paris {#métropole_du_grand_paris}

![A map of the Greater Paris Metropolis and its governing
territories](Établissements_publics_territoriaux_du_Grand_Paris_et_trois_départements.svg "A map of the Greater Paris Metropolis and its governing territories")
In January 2016, the [Métropole du Grand Paris](Grand_Paris "wikilink")
came into existence.[^55] It is an administrative structure for
co-operation between the City of Paris and its nearest suburbs. It
includes the City of Paris, plus the communes of the three departments
of the inner suburbs, [Hauts-de-Seine](Hauts-de-Seine "wikilink"),
[Seine-Saint-Denis](Seine-Saint-Denis "wikilink") and
[Val-de-Marne](Val-de-Marne "wikilink"), plus seven communes in the
outer suburbs, including [Argenteuil](Argenteuil "wikilink") in [Val
d\'Oise](Val_d'Oise "wikilink") and
[Paray-Vieille-Poste](Paray-Vieille-Poste "wikilink") in
[Essonne](Essonne "wikilink"), which were added to include the major
airports of Paris. The Metropole covers
`{{cvt|814|km2|sqmi|0|abbr=off}}`{=mediawiki}. In 2015, it had a
population of 6.945 million people.[^56][^57]

The new structure is administered by a Metropolitan Council of 210
members, not directly elected, but chosen by the councils of the member
Communes. By 2020, its basic competencies will include urban planning,
housing, and protection of the environment.[^58][^59] In January 2016,
[Patrick Ollier](Patrick_Ollier "wikilink") was elected the first
president of the metropolitan council. Though the Metropole has a
population of nearly seven million people and accounts for 25 percent of
the GDP of France, it has a very small budget: just 65 million Euros,
compared with eight billion Euros for the City of Paris.[^60]

### Regional government {#regional_government}

The [Region](Region "wikilink") of [Île de
France](Île_de_France "wikilink"), including Paris and its surrounding
communities, is governed by the [Regional
Council](Regional_Council_of_Île-de-France "wikilink"), composed of 209
members representing its different communes. In December 2015, a list of
candidates of the Union of the Right, a coalition of centrist and
right-wing parties, led by [Valérie
Pécresse](Valérie_Pécresse "wikilink"), narrowly won the regional
election, defeating a coalition of Socialists and ecologists. The
Socialists had governed the region for seventeen years. The regional
council has 121 members from the Union of the Right, 66 from the Union
of the Left and 22 from the extreme right National Front.[^61]

### National government {#national_government}

![The [Élysée Palace](Élysée_Palace "wikilink"), official residence of
the [President of
France](President_of_France "wikilink")](<Secretary_Pompeo_Arrives_to_Meet_with_French_Foreign_Minister_Le_Drian_in_Paris_(50610423656).jpg> "The Élysée Palace, official residence of the President of France")
As the capital of France, Paris is the seat of France\'s [national
government](Government_of_France "wikilink"). For the executive, the two
chief officers each have their own official residences, which also serve
as their offices. The [President of the French
Republic](President_of_France "wikilink") resides at the [Élysée
Palace](Élysée_Palace "wikilink").[^62] The [Prime
Minister](Prime_Minister_of_France "wikilink")\'s seat is at the [Hôtel
Matignon](Hôtel_Matignon "wikilink").[^63]`{{sfn|Knapp|Wright|2006|pp=93–94}}`{=mediawiki}
Government ministries are located in various parts of the city, many
near the Hôtel Matignon.`{{sfn|Borrus|2012|p=288}}`{=mediawiki}

Both houses of the [French Parliament](French_Parliament "wikilink") are
located on the Rive Gauche. The upper house, the
[Senate](<Senate_(France)> "wikilink"), meets in the [Palais du
Luxembourg](Palais_du_Luxembourg "wikilink"). The more important lower
house, the [National Assembly](<National_Assembly_(France)> "wikilink"),
meets in the [Palais Bourbon](Palais_Bourbon "wikilink"). The [President
of the Senate](List_of_presidents_of_the_Senate_of_France "wikilink"),
the second-highest public official in France, with the President of the
Republic being the sole superior, resides in the [Petit
Luxembourg](Petit_Luxembourg "wikilink"), a smaller palace annexe to the
Palais du Luxembourg.[^64]

![The Palais-Royal, residence of the [Conseil
d'État](Conseil_d'État_(France) "wikilink")](Conseil_d'Etat_Paris_WA.jpg "The Palais-Royal, residence of the Conseil d'État")

France\'s highest courts are located in Paris. The [Court of
Cassation](<Court_of_Cassation_(France)> "wikilink"), the highest court in
the judicial order, which reviews criminal and civil cases, is located
in the [Palais de Justice](Palais_de_Justice,_Paris "wikilink") on the
_Île de la Cité_.[^65] The [Conseil
d\'État](<Council_of_State_(France)> "wikilink"), which provides legal
advice to the executive and acts as the highest court in the
administrative order, judging litigation against public bodies, is
located in the [Palais-Royal](Palais-Royal "wikilink") in the [1st
arrondissement](1st_arrondissement_of_Paris "wikilink").[^66] The
[Constitutional Council](Constitutional_Council_of_France "wikilink"),
an advisory body with ultimate authority on the constitutionality of
laws and government decrees, meets in the Montpensier wing of the Palais
Royal.[^67]

Paris and its region host the headquarters of several international
organisations, including [UNESCO](UNESCO "wikilink"), the [Organisation
for Economic Co-operation and
Development](Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development "wikilink"),
the [International Chamber of
Commerce](International_Chamber_of_Commerce "wikilink"), the [Paris
Club](Paris_Club "wikilink"), the [European Space
Agency](European_Space_Agency "wikilink"), the [International Energy
Agency](International_Energy_Agency "wikilink"), the _[Organisation
internationale de la
Francophonie](Organisation_internationale_de_la_Francophonie "wikilink")_,
the [European Union Institute for Security
Studies](European_Union_Institute_for_Security_Studies "wikilink"), the
[International Bureau of Weights and
Measures](International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures "wikilink"), the
[International Exhibition
Bureau](Bureau_of_International_Expositions "wikilink"), and the
[International Federation for Human
Rights](International_Federation_for_Human_Rights "wikilink").

### Police force {#police_force}

![[Gendarmerie](National_Gendarmerie "wikilink")
motorcyclists](Gendarmerie_BMW_R1100RT.jpg "Gendarmerie motorcyclists")
The security of Paris is mainly the responsibility of the [Prefecture of
Police of Paris](Prefecture_of_Police_of_Paris "wikilink"), a
subdivision of the [Ministry of the
Interior](<Minister_of_the_Interior_(France)> "wikilink"). It supervises
the units of the [National Police](<National_Police_(France)> "wikilink")
who patrol the city and the three neighbouring departments. It is also
responsible for providing emergency services, including the [Paris Fire
Brigade](Paris_Fire_Brigade "wikilink"). Its headquarters is on [Place
Louis Lépine](Place_Louis_Lépine "wikilink") on the [Île de la
Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink").[^68]

There are 43,800 officers under the prefecture, and a fleet of more than
6,000 vehicles, including police cars, motorcycles, fire trucks, boats,
and helicopters.[^69] The national police has its own special unit for
riot control and crowd control, and security of public buildings, called
the [Compagnies Républicaines de
Sécurité](Compagnies_Républicaines_de_Sécurité "wikilink") (CRS). Vans
of CRS agents are frequently seen in the centre of Paris when there are
demonstrations and public events. The police are supported by the
[National Gendarmerie](National_Gendarmerie "wikilink"), a branch of the
[French Armed Forces](French_Armed_Forces "wikilink"). Their police
operations are supervised by the Ministry of the Interior.[^70]

Crime in Paris is similar to that in most large cities. Violent crime is
relatively rare in the city centre. Political violence is uncommon,
though very large demonstrations may occur in Paris and other French
cities simultaneously. These demonstrations, usually managed by a strong
police presence, can turn confrontational and escalate into
violence.[^71]

### Urbanism and architecture {#urbanism_and_architecture}

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Architecture of Paris|Haussmann's renovation of Paris|Religious buildings in Paris|List of tallest buildings and structures in the Paris region}}
```

![The [Rue de
Rivoli](Rue_de_Rivoli "wikilink")](Rue_de_Rivoli_at_night,_Paris_August_2013.jpg "The Rue de Rivoli")
![The [Place des Vosges](Place_des_Vosges "wikilink"), the oldest
planned square in
Paris](Paris_Place_des_Vosges_02.jpg "The Place des Vosges, the oldest planned square in Paris")
Paris is one of the few world capitals that has rarely seen destruction
by catastrophe or war. As a result, even its earliest history is visible
in its street map, and centuries of rulers adding their respective
architectural marks on the capital have resulted in an accumulated
wealth of history-rich monuments and buildings whose beauty plays a
large part in giving Paris the reputation it has today.[^72] At its
origin, before the Middle Ages, Paris was composed of several islands
and sandbanks in a bend of the [Seine](Seine "wikilink"). Of those, two
remain today: [Île Saint-Louis](Île_Saint-Louis "wikilink") and the [Île
de la Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink"). A third one is the 1827
artificially created [Île aux Cygnes](Île_aux_Cygnes "wikilink").

Modern Paris owes much of its downtown plan and architectural harmony to
[Napoleon III](Napoleon_III "wikilink") and his Prefect of the Seine,
[Baron Haussmann](Georges-Eugène_Haussmann "wikilink"). Between [1853
and 1870 they rebuilt the city
centre](Haussmann's_renovation_of_Paris "wikilink"), created the wide
downtown boulevards and squares where the boulevards intersected,
imposed standard facades along the boulevards, and required that the
facades be built of the distinctive cream-grey \"[Paris
stone](Paris_stone "wikilink")\". They built the major parks around
central Paris.[^73] The high residential population of the city centre
makes Paris much different from most other major western
cities.`{{sfn|Braimoh|Vlek|2008|p=12}}`{=mediawiki}

Paris\'s urbanism laws have been under strict control since the early
17th century,[^74] particularly where street-front alignment, building
height and building distribution is concerned.[^75] The
`{{cvt|210|m}}`{=mediawiki} [Tour
Montparnasse](Tour_Montparnasse "wikilink") was both Paris\'s and
France\'s tallest building from 1973 until 2011,[^76] when the record
was taken by the [La Défense](La_Défense "wikilink") district\'s [Tour
First](Tour_First "wikilink") in the commune of
[Courbevoie](Courbevoie "wikilink").

### Housing

![[Front de Seine](Front_de_Seine "wikilink") development along the
river
[Seine](Seine "wikilink")](Front_de_Seine_-_Paris_15.jpg "Front de Seine development along the river Seine")

In 2018, the most expensive residential street in Paris by average price
per square metre was [Avenue Montaigne](Avenue_Montaigne "wikilink"), at
22,372 euros per square metre.[^77] In 2011, the number of residences in
the City of Paris was `{{formatnum:1356074}}`{=mediawiki}. Among these,
`{{formatnum:1165541}}`{=mediawiki} (85.9 percent) were main residences,
`{{formatnum:91835}}`{=mediawiki} (6.8 percent) were secondary
residences, and the remaining 7.3 percent were empty.[^78]

Sixty-two percent of buildings date from 1949 and before, with 20
percent built between 1949 and 1974. 18 percent of Paris buildings were
built after 1974.[^79] Two-thirds of the city\'s 1.3 million residences
are studio and two-room apartments. Paris averages 1.9 people per
residence, a number that has remained constant since the 1980s, which is
less than Île-de-France\'s 2.33 people-per-residence average. Only 33
percent of Parisian principal residents own their habitation, against 47
percent for the wider Île-de-France region. Most of Paris\'s population
rents their residence.[^80] In 2017, social or public housing was 19.9
percent of Paris\'s residences. Its distribution varies widely
throughout Paris, from 2.6 percent of the housing in the wealthy 7th
arrondissement to 39.9 percent in the 19th arrondissement.[^81] ![19th
Arrondissement Residential
Area](Paris_19th_Arrondissement_Residential_Area.jpg "19th Arrondissement Residential Area"){width="225"}

In February 2019, a Paris NGO conducted its annual citywide count of
homeless persons. They counted 3,641 homeless persons in Paris, of whom
twelve percent were women. More than half had been homeless for more
than a year. 2,885 were living in the streets or parks, 298 in train and
metro stations, and 756 in other forms of temporary shelter. This was an
increase of 588 persons since 2018.[^82]

### Suburbs

![Western Paris in 2016, photographed by a [SkySat](SkySat "wikilink")
satellite](Paris,_France,_September_24,_2016_SkySat.jpg "Western Paris in 2016, photographed by a SkySat satellite")
![West of Paris seen from [Tour
Montparnasse](Tour_Montparnasse "wikilink"),
2019](West_of_Paris_seen_from_Tour_Montparnasse_-_2019-09-18.jpg "West of Paris seen from Tour Montparnasse, 2019")
Aside from the 20th-century addition of the Bois de Boulogne, the Bois
de Vincennes, and the Paris heliport, Paris\'s administrative limits
have remained unchanged since 1860. A greater administrative
[Seine](<Seine_(department)> "wikilink") department had been governing
Paris and its suburbs since its creation in 1790, but the rising
suburban population had made it difficult to maintain as a unique
entity. To address this problem, the parent \"District de la région
parisienne\" (\'district of the Paris region\') was reorganised into
several new departments from 1968: Paris became a department in itself,
and the administration of its suburbs was divided between the three new
departments surrounding it. The district of the Paris region was renamed
\"[Île-de-France](Île-de-France "wikilink")\" in 1977, but this
abbreviated \"Paris region\" name is still commonly used today to
describe the Île-de-France, and as a vague reference to the entire Paris
agglomeration.[^83] Long-intended measures to unite Paris with its
suburbs began in January 2016, when the Métropole du [Grand
Paris](Grand_Paris "wikilink") came into existence.[^84]

Paris\'s disconnect with its suburbs, its lack of suburban
transportation, in particular, became all too apparent with the Paris
agglomeration\'s growth. [Paul Delouvrier](Paul_Delouvrier "wikilink")
promised to resolve the Paris-suburbs _mésentente_ when he became head
of the Paris region in 1961.`{{sfn|Masson|1984|p=536}}`{=mediawiki} Two
of his most ambitious projects for the Region were the construction of
five suburban \"villes nouvelles\" (\"new
cities\")`{{sfn|Yarri|2008|p=407}}`{=mediawiki} and the
[RER](Réseau_Express_Régional "wikilink") commuter train
network.`{{sfn|Gordon|2006|pp=46–47}}`{=mediawiki}

Many other suburban residential districts (_grands ensembles_) were
built between the 1960s and 1970s to provide a low-cost solution for a
rapidly expanding population.`{{sfn|Castells|1983|p=75}}`{=mediawiki}
These districts were socially mixed at
first,`{{sfn|Tomas|Blanc|Bonilla|IERP|2003|p=237}}`{=mediawiki} but few
residents actually owned their homes. The growing economy made these
accessible to the middle classes only from the 1970s.[^85] Their poor
construction quality and their haphazard insertion into existing urban
growth contributed to their desertion by those able to move elsewhere,
and their repopulation by those with more limited resources.[^86]

These areas, _quartiers sensibles_ (\"sensitive quarters\"), are in
northern and eastern Paris, namely around its [Goutte
d\'Or](Goutte_d'Or "wikilink") and
[Belleville](Belleville,_Paris "wikilink") neighbourhoods. To the north
of Paris, they are grouped mainly in the
[Seine-Saint-Denis](Seine-Saint-Denis "wikilink")
[department](<department_(France)> "wikilink"), and to a lesser extent, to
the east in the [Val-d\'Oise](Val-d'Oise "wikilink")
[department](<department_(France)> "wikilink"). Other difficult areas are
located in the [Seine](Seine "wikilink") valley, in
[Évry](<Évry_(Essonne)> "wikilink") et
[Corbeil-Essonnes](Corbeil-Essonnes "wikilink")
([Essonne](<Essonne_(département)> "wikilink")), in
[Mureaux](Les_Mureaux "wikilink"),
[Mantes-la-Jolie](Mantes-la-Jolie "wikilink")
([Yvelines](Yvelines "wikilink")), and scattered among social housing
districts created by Delouvrier\'s 1961 \"ville nouvelle\" political
initiative.[^87]

The Paris agglomeration\'s [urban sociology](urban_sociology "wikilink")
is basically that of 19th-century Paris: the wealthy live in the west
and southwest, and the middle-to-working classes are in the north and
east. The remaining areas are mostly middle-class, dotted with wealthy
islands in areas of historical importance, namely
[Saint-Maur-des-Fossés](Saint-Maur-des-Fossés "wikilink") to the east
and [Enghien-les-Bains](Enghien-les-Bains "wikilink") to the north of
Paris.[^88]

## Demographics

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Demographics of Paris}}
```

![City of Paris population pyramid in
2022](Paris_population_pyramid.svg "City of Paris population pyramid in 2022")
`{{Collapsible Table Paris Region top countries & territories of birth}}`{=mediawiki}
The population of the City of Paris was 2,102,650 in January 2023, down
from 2,165,423 in January 2022, according to the
[INSEE](Institut_national_de_la_statistique_et_des_études_économiques "wikilink"),
the French statistical agency. Between 2013 and 2023, the population
fell by 122,919, or about five percent. The Mayor of Paris, [Anne
Hidalgo](Anne_Hidalgo "wikilink"), declared that this illustrated the
\"de-densification\" of the city, creating more green space and less
crowding.[^89][^90] Despite the drop, Paris remains the most densely
populated city in Europe, with 252 residents per hectare, not counting
parks.[^91] This drop was attributed partly to a lower birth rate, the
departure of middle-class residents, and the possible loss of housing in
Paris, due to short-term rentals for tourism.[^92]

Paris is the fourth largest municipality in the European Union, after
[Berlin](Berlin "wikilink"), [Madrid](Madrid "wikilink") and
[Rome](Rome "wikilink"), and is the [34th most densely populated city in
the world](List_of_cities_proper_by_population_density "wikilink")
`{{as of|2020|lc=y}}`{=mediawiki}.[^93] [Eurostat](Eurostat "wikilink")
places Paris (6.5 million people) behind London (8 million) and ahead of
Berlin (3.5 million), based on the 2012 populations of what Eurostat
calls \"urban audit core cities\".[^94] The population of Paris today is
lower than its historical peak of 2.9 million in 1921.[^95] The
principal reasons are a significant decline in household size and a
dramatic migration of residents to the suburbs between 1962 and 1975.
Factors in the migration included
[de-industrialisation](de-industrialisation "wikilink"), high rent, the
[gentrification](gentrification "wikilink") of many inner quarters, the
transformation of living space into offices, and greater affluence among
working families. Paris\'s population loss came to a temporary halt at
the beginning of the 21st century. The population increased from
2,125,246 in 1999 to 2,240,621 in 2012, before declining again slightly
in 2017, 2018, and again in 2021.[^96][^97]

Paris is the core of a built-up area that extends well beyond its
limits: commonly referred to as the _agglomération Parisienne_, and
statistically as a _[unité urbaine](unité_urbaine "wikilink")_ (a
measure of [urban area](urban_area "wikilink")), the Paris
agglomeration\'s population of 10,785,092 in 2017 made it the [largest
urban area in the European
Union](List_of_urban_areas_in_the_European_Union "wikilink").[^98][^99]
City-influenced commuter activity reaches further, in a statistical
[_aire d\'attraction_ de Paris](Paris_Metropolitan_Area "wikilink"),
(\"functional area\", a statistical method comparable to a [metropolitan
area](metropolitan_area "wikilink")),[^100] that had a population of
13,024,518 in 2017,[^101] 19.6% of the population of France,[^102] and
the [largest metropolitan
area](List_of_metropolitan_areas_in_Europe "wikilink") in the
[Eurozone](Eurozone "wikilink").[^103]

In 2012, according to [Eurostat](Eurostat "wikilink"), the EU
statistical agency, 2012 the Commune of Paris was the most densely
populated city in the European Union. There were 21,616 people per
square kilometre within the city limits, the NUTS-3 statistical area,
ahead of Inner London West, which had 10,374 people per square
kilometre. In the same census, three departments bordering Paris,
[Hauts-de-Seine](Hauts-de-Seine "wikilink"),
[Seine-Saint-Denis](Seine-Saint-Denis "wikilink") and
[Val-de-Marne](Val-de-Marne "wikilink"), had population densities of
over 10,000 people per square kilometre, ranking among the 10 most
densely populated areas of the
EU.[^104]
`{{verify source|date=June 2019 |reason=Old data, 2012 does not match with report date (March 2014). The current report could be different.}}`

### Migration

Under French law, people born in foreign countries with no French
citizenship at birth are defined as immigrants. In the 2012 census,
135,853 residents of the City of Paris were immigrants from
[Europe](Europe "wikilink"), 112,369 were immigrants from the
[Maghreb](Maghreb "wikilink"), 70,852 from [sub-Saharan
Africa](sub-Saharan_Africa "wikilink") and [Egypt](Egypt "wikilink"),
5,059 from [Turkey](Turkey "wikilink"), 91,297 from
[Asia](Asia "wikilink") outside Turkey, 38,858 from the
[Americas](Americas "wikilink"), and 1,365 from the [South
Pacific](Oceania "wikilink").[^105]

In the Paris Region, 590,504 residents were immigrants from Europe,
627,078 were immigrants from the Maghreb, 435,339 from sub-Saharan
Africa and Egypt, 69,338 from Turkey, 322,330 from Asia outside Turkey,
113,363 from the Americas, and 2,261 from the South Pacific.[^106]

In 2012, there were 8,810 British citizens and 10,019 United States
citizens living in the City of Paris (Ville de Paris), and 20,466
British citizens and 16,408 United States citizens living in the entire
Paris Region ([Île-de-France](Île-de-France "wikilink")).[^107][^108]

In 2020--2021, about 6 million people, or 41% of the population of the
Paris Region, were either immigrants (21%) or had at least one immigrant
parent (20%). These figures do not include French people born in
Overseas France and their direct descendants.[^109]

### Religion

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Religious buildings in Paris}}
```

![[Sacré-Cœur](Sacré-Cœur,_Paris "wikilink") in
[Montmartre](Montmartre "wikilink")](Le_sacre_coeur.jpg "Sacré-Cœur in Montmartre")

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Paris was the largest
[Catholic](Catholic_Church "wikilink") city in the world.[^110] French
census data does not contain information about religious
affiliation.[^111] In a 2011 survey by the [Institut français d\'opinion
publique](Institut_français_d'opinion_publique "wikilink") (IFOP), a
French public opinion research organisation, 61 percent of residents of
the Paris Region (Île-de-France) identified themselves as [Roman
Catholic](Catholic_Church "wikilink"). In the same survey, 7 percent of
residents identified themselves as Muslims, 4 percent as Protestants, 2
percent as Jewish, and 25 percent as without religion.

According to the INSEE, between 4 and 5 million French residents were
born, or had at least one parent born, in a predominantly [Muslim
country](Islam_in_France "wikilink"), particularly
[Algeria](Algeria "wikilink"), [Morocco](Morocco "wikilink"), and
[Tunisia](Tunisia "wikilink"). An IFOP survey in 2008 reported that, of
immigrants from these predominantly Muslim countries, 25 percent went to
the mosque regularly. 41 percent practised the religion, and 34 percent
were believers, but did not practice the religion.[^112][^113] In 2012
and 2013, it was estimated that there were almost 500,000 Muslims in the
City of Paris, 1.5 million Muslims in the Île-de-France region and 4 to
5 million Muslims in France.[^114][^115]

In 2014, the Jewish population of the Paris Region was estimated to be
282,000, the largest concentration of Jews in the world outside of
[Israel](Israel "wikilink") and the United States.[^116]

## Economy

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Economy of Paris}}
```

![[La Défense](La_Défense "wikilink"), the largest dedicated business
district in Europe, located to the west of
Paris[^117]](<La_Défense_depuis_l'Arc_de_Triomphe_Janvier_2023_(cropped).jpg> "La Défense, the largest dedicated business district in Europe, located to the west of Paris")
![The [headquarters](headquarters "wikilink") of [BNP
Paribas](BNP_Paribas "wikilink"), the largest banking group in Europe,
in the [Boulevard des
Italiens](Boulevard_des_Italiens "wikilink").[^119]](Italiens12.jpg "The headquarters of BNP Paribas, the largest banking group in Europe, in the Boulevard des Italiens.")
![[Axa](Axa "wikilink") headquarters at Hôtel de La
Vaupalière](Hotel_de_la_vaupaliere54.jpg "Axa headquarters at Hôtel de La Vaupalière")
![The `{{lang|fr|[[Crédit Agricole]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki}
headquarters in [Montrouge](Montrouge "wikilink"), south of
Paris[^121]](Campus_Evergreen.jpg "The  headquarters in Montrouge, south of Paris")
The economy of the City of Paris is based largely on services and
commerce. Of the 390,480 enterprises in Paris, 80.6 percent are engaged
in commerce, transportation, and diverse services, 6.5 percent in
construction, and 3.8 percent in industry.[^123] The story is similar in
the [Paris Region](Paris_Region "wikilink") (Île-de-France): 76.7
percent of enterprises are engaged in commerce and services, and 3.4
percent in industry.[^124]

At the 2012 census, 59.5% of jobs in the Paris Region were in market
services (12.0% in wholesale and retail trade, 9.7% in professional,
scientific, and technical services, 6.5% in information and
communication, 6.5% in transportation and warehousing, 5.9% in finance
and insurance, 5.8% in administrative and support services, 4.6% in
accommodation and food services, and 8.5% in various other market
services), 26.9% in non-market services (10.4% in human health and
social work activities, 9.6% in public administration and defence, and
6.9% in education), 8.2% in manufacturing and utilities (6.6% in
manufacturing and 1.5% in utilities), 5.2% in construction, and 0.2% in
agriculture.[^125][^126]

The Paris Region had 5.4 million salaried employees in 2010, of whom 2.2
million were concentrated in 39 _pôles d\'emplois_ or business
districts. The largest of these by number of employees is known in
French as the QCA, or _quartier central des affaires_. In 2010, it was
the workplace of 500,000 salaried employees, about 30 percent of the
salaried employees in Paris and 10 percent of those in the
Île-de-France. The largest sectors of activity in the central business
district were finance and insurance (16 percent of employees in the
district) and business services (15 percent). The district includes a
large concentration of department stores, shopping areas, hotels and
restaurants, as well a government offices and ministries.[^127]

The second-largest business district in terms of employment is [La
Défense](La_Défense "wikilink"), just west of the city. In 2010, it was
the workplace of 144,600 employees, of whom 38 percent worked in finance
and insurance, 16 percent in business support services. Two other
important districts, [Neuilly-sur-Seine](Neuilly-sur-Seine "wikilink")
and [Levallois-Perret](Levallois-Perret "wikilink"), are extensions of
the Paris business district and of La Défense. Another district,
including [Boulogne-Billancourt](Boulogne-Billancourt "wikilink"),
[Issy-les-Moulineaux](Issy-les-Moulineaux "wikilink") and the southern
part of the 15th arrondissement, is a centre of activity for the media
and information technology.[^128]

In 2021, the top French companies listed in the [Fortune Global
500](Fortune_Global_500 "wikilink") all have their headquarters in the
Paris Region. Six are in the central business district of the City of
Paris, four are close to the city in the
[Hauts-de-Seine](Hauts-de-Seine "wikilink") Department, three are in [La
Défense](La_Défense "wikilink"), and one is in
[Boulogne-Billancourt](Boulogne-Billancourt "wikilink"). Some companies,
like [Société Générale](Société_Générale "wikilink"), have offices in
both Paris and La Défense. The Paris Region is France\'s leading region
for economic activity, with a [GDP](Gross_domestic_product "wikilink")
of [€](euro "wikilink")865 billion, of which €280 billion was in Paris
city.[^129] In 2021, its GDP ranked first among the metropolitan regions
of the EU, and its per-capita GDP PPP was the 8th
highest.[^130][^131][^132] While the Paris region\'s population
accounted for 18.8 percent of metropolitan France in 2019,[^133] the
Paris region\'s GDP accounted for 32 percent of metropolitan France\'s
GDP.[^134][^135]

The Paris Region economy has gradually shifted from industry to
high-value-added service industries
([finance](Financial_services "wikilink"), IT services) and high-tech
manufacturing (electronics, optics, aerospace, etc.).[^136] The Paris
region\'s most intense economic activity through the central
[Hauts-de-Seine](Hauts-de-Seine "wikilink") department and suburban La
Défense business district places Paris\'s economic centre to the west of
the city, in a triangle between the
`{{lang|fr|[[Palais Garnier|Opéra Garnier]]}}`{=mediawiki}, _La Défense_
and the _[Val de Seine](Val_de_Seine "wikilink")_.[^137] While the Paris
economy is dominated by [services](Service_Sector "wikilink"), and
employment in manufacturing sector has declined sharply, the region
remains an important manufacturing centre, particularly for aeronautics,
automobiles, and eco industries.[^138]

In the 2017 worldwide cost of living survey by the [Economist
Intelligence Unit](Economist_Intelligence_Unit "wikilink"), based on a
survey made in September 2016, Paris ranked as the seventh most
expensive city in the world, and the second most expensive in
Europe.[^139] In 2018, Paris was the most expensive city in the world
along with [Singapore](Singapore "wikilink") and [Hong
Kong](Hong_Kong "wikilink").[^140] [Station F](Station_F "wikilink") is
a [business incubator](business_incubator "wikilink") for startups,
noted as the world\'s largest startup facility.[^141] In 2022, Paris was
the city with the ninth-highest cost of living in the world.[^142]

### Employment and income {#employment_and_income}

![The median income in Paris and its nearest departments, 2018. High
income in red, low income in
yellow](<Median_income_of_Paris_and_inner_ring_(2018)_named.svg> "The median income in Paris and its nearest departments, 2018. High income in red, low income in yellow")
In 2007, the majority of Paris\'s salaried employees filled 370,000
businesses services jobs, concentrated in the north-western 8th, 16th
and 17th arrondissements.[^143] Paris\'s financial service companies are
concentrated in the central-western 8th and 9th arrondissement banking
and insurance district.[^144] Paris\'s department store district in the
1st, 6th, 8th, and 9th arrondissements employs ten percent of mostly
female Paris workers, with 100,000 of these in the retail trade.[^145]
Fourteen percent of Parisians worked in hotels and restaurants and other
services to individuals.[^146]

Nineteen percent of Paris employees work for the State in either
administration or education. The majority of Paris\'s healthcare and
social workers work at the hospitals and social housing, concentrated in
the peripheral 13th, 14th, 18th, 19th, and 20th arrondissements.[^147]
Outside Paris, the western Hauts-de-Seine department [La
Défense](La_Défense "wikilink") district specialising in finance,
insurance, and scientific research, employs 144,600.[^148] The
north-eastern Seine-Saint-Denis audiovisual sector has 200 media firms
and 10 major film studios.[^149]

Paris\'s manufacturing is mostly focused in its suburbs. Paris has
around 75,000 manufacturing workers, most of whom are in the textile,
clothing, leather goods, and shoe trades.[^150] In 2015, the Paris
region\'s 800 aerospace companies employed 100,000.[^151] Four hundred
automobile industry companies employ another 100,000 workers. Many of
these are centred in the [Yvelines](Yvelines "wikilink") department,
around the Renault and PSA-Citroën plants. This department alone employs
33,000.[^152] In 2014, the industry as a whole suffered a major loss,
with the closing of a major
[Aulnay-sous-Bois](Aulnay-sous-Bois "wikilink") Citroën assembly
plant.[^153]

The southern [Essonne](Essonne "wikilink") department specialises in
science and technology.[^154] The south-eastern
[Val-de-Marne](Val-de-Marne "wikilink"), with its wholesale [Rungis food
market](Marché_international_de_Rungis "wikilink"), specialises in food
processing and beverages.[^155] The Paris region\'s manufacturing
decline is quickly being replaced by eco-industries. These employ about
100,000 workers.[^156]

Incomes are higher in the Western part of Paris and in the western
suburbs, than in the northern and eastern parts of the urban area.[^157]
While Paris has some of the richest neighbourhoods in France, it also
has some of the poorest, mostly on the eastern side of the city. In
2012, 14 percent of households in Paris earned less than €977 per month,
the official [poverty line](poverty_line "wikilink"). Twenty-five
percent of residents in the 19th arrondissement lived below the poverty
line. In Paris\'s wealthiest neighbourhood, the 7th arrondissement, 7
percent lived below the poverty line.[^158] The unemployment rate in
Paris in the 4th trimester of 2021 was six percent, compared with 7.4
percent in the whole of France. This was the lowest rate in thirteen
years.[^159][^160]

As of 2024, Paris was considered the most attractive city in the world

[Greater Paris](Grand_Paris "wikilink"), comprising Paris and its three
surrounding departments, received a record 38 million visitors in 2019,

visitors. Of the foreign visitors, the greatest number came from the
United States (2.6 million), the United Kingdom (1.2 million), Germany
(981 thousand), and China (711 thousand).[^164]

In 2018, measured by the [Euromonitor](Euromonitor "wikilink") Global

destination in the world, with 19.10 million visitors, behind Bangkok
(22.78 million) but ahead of London (19.09 million).[^165] In 2016,

d\'Orsay](Musée_d'Orsay "wikilink") (3.27 million visitors) and the
[Centre Pompidou](Centre_Pompidou "wikilink") (3 million
visitors).[^167]

In 2019, Greater Paris had 2,056 hotels, including 94 five-star hotels,
with a total of 121,646 rooms.[^168] In 2019, in addition to the hotels,
Greater Paris had 60,000 homes registered with
[Airbnb](Airbnb "wikilink").[^169] Under French law, renters of these
units must pay the Paris tourism tax. The company paid the city
government 7.3 million euros in 2016.[^170]

A minuscule fraction of foreign visitors suffer from [Paris
syndrome](Paris_syndrome "wikilink"), when their experiences do not meet
expectations.[^171]

## Culture

### Painting and sculpture {#painting_and_sculpture}

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Art in Paris}}
```

![[Auguste Renoir](Auguste_Renoir "wikilink"), *[Bal du moulin de la
Galette](Bal_du_moulin_de_la_Galette "wikilink")*, 1876, oil on canvas,
`{{cvt|131|x|175|cm|0}}`{=mediawiki}, [Musée
d'Orsay](Musée_d'Orsay "wikilink")](Renoir,_Pierre-Auguste_-_Dance_at_Le_Moulin_de_la_Galette,_1876.jpg "Auguste Renoir, Bal du moulin de la Galette, 1876, oil on canvas, , Musée d'Orsay")
For centuries, Paris has attracted artists from around the world. As a
result, Paris has acquired a reputation as the \"City of
Art\".`{{sfn|Montclos|2003}}`{=mediawiki} Italian artists were a
profound influence on the development of art in Paris in the 16th and
17th centuries, particularly in sculpture and reliefs. Painting and
sculpture became the pride of the French monarchy, and the French royal
family commissioned many Parisian artists to adorn their palaces during
the [French Baroque and
Classicism](French_Baroque_and_Classicism "wikilink") era. Sculptors
such as [Girardon](François_Girardon "wikilink"),
[Coysevox](Antoine_Coysevox "wikilink"), and
[Coustou](Nicolas_Coustou "wikilink") acquired reputations as the finest
artists in the royal court in 17th-century France. [Pierre
Mignard](Pierre_Mignard "wikilink") became the first painter to King
[Louis XIV](Louis_XIV "wikilink") during this period. In 1648, the
_[Académie royale de peinture et de
sculpture](Académie_royale_de_peinture_et_de_sculpture "wikilink")_
(Royal Academy of Painting and Sculpture) was established to accommodate
the dramatic interest in art in the
capital.`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}

Paris was in its artistic prime in the 19th century and early 20th
century, when it had a colony of artists established in the city and in
art schools associated with some of the finest painters of the times:
[Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec](Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec "wikilink"),
[Édouard Manet](Édouard_Manet "wikilink"), [Claude
Monet](Claude_Monet "wikilink"), [Berthe
Morisot](Berthe_Morisot "wikilink"), [Paul
Gauguin](Paul_Gauguin "wikilink"), [Pierre-Auguste
Renoir](Pierre-Auguste_Renoir "wikilink") and others. Paris was central
to the development of [Romanticism](Romanticism "wikilink") in art, with
painters such as
[Géricault](Théodore_Géricault "wikilink").`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}
[Impressionism](Impressionism "wikilink"), [Art
Nouveau](Art_Nouveau "wikilink"),
[Symbolism](<Symbolism_(arts)> "wikilink"), [Fauvism](Fauvism "wikilink"),
[Cubism](Cubism "wikilink") and [Art Deco](Art_Deco "wikilink")
movements all evolved in Paris.`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki} In
the late 19th century, many artists in the French provinces and
worldwide flocked to Paris to exhibit their works in the numerous salons
and expositions and make a name for
themselves.`{{sfn|Perry|1995|p=19}}`{=mediawiki} Artists such as [Pablo
Picasso](Pablo_Picasso "wikilink"), [Henri
Matisse](Henri_Matisse "wikilink"), [Vincent van
Gogh](Vincent_van_Gogh "wikilink"), [Paul
Cézanne](Paul_Cézanne "wikilink"), [Jean
Metzinger](Jean_Metzinger "wikilink"), [Albert
Gleizes](Albert_Gleizes "wikilink"), [Henri
Rousseau](Henri_Rousseau "wikilink"), [Marc
Chagall](Marc_Chagall "wikilink"), [Amedeo
Modigliani](Amedeo_Modigliani "wikilink") became associated with Paris.

The most prestigious sculptors who made their reputation in Paris in the
modern era are [Frédéric Auguste
Bartholdi](Frédéric_Auguste_Bartholdi "wikilink") ([Statue of
Liberty](Statue_of_Liberty "wikilink")), [Auguste
Rodin](Auguste_Rodin "wikilink"), [Camille
Claudel](Camille_Claudel "wikilink"), [Antoine
Bourdelle](Antoine_Bourdelle "wikilink"), [Paul
Landowski](Paul_Landowski "wikilink") ([_Christ the
Redeemer_](<Christ_the_Redeemer_(statue)> "wikilink") in [Rio de
Janeiro](Rio_de_Janeiro "wikilink")) and [Aristide
Maillol](Aristide_Maillol "wikilink"). The [Golden
Age](Belle_Époque "wikilink") of the [School of
Paris](School_of_Paris "wikilink") ended between the two world wars.

The [Louvre](Louvre "wikilink") received 2.8 million visitors in
2021,[^172] holding its position as the [world\'s most-visited

### Theatre

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Theatre of France}}
```

The largest opera houses of Paris are the 19th-century Opéra Garnier
(historical [Paris Opéra](Opéra_National_de_Paris "wikilink")) and
modern [Opéra Bastille](Opéra_Bastille "wikilink"); the former tends
toward the more classic ballets and operas, and the latter provides a
mixed repertoire of classic and
modern.`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=178}}`{=mediawiki} In the
mid-19th century, there were three other active and competing opera
houses: the Opéra-Comique (which still exists),
[Théâtre-Italien](Théâtre-Italien "wikilink") and [Théâtre
Lyrique](Théâtre_Lyrique "wikilink") (now the [Théâtre de la
Ville](Théâtre_de_la_Ville "wikilink")).`{{Sfn|Schumacher|1996|p=60}}`{=mediawiki}
[Philharmonie de Paris](Philharmonie_de_Paris "wikilink"), the modern
symphonic concert hall of Paris, opened in January 2015. Another musical
landmark is the [Théâtre des
Champs-Élysées](Théâtre_des_Champs-Élysées "wikilink"), where the first
performances of Diaghilev\'s [Ballets Russes](Ballets_Russes "wikilink")
took place in 1913. ![The [Comédie
Française](Comédie_Française "wikilink") (Salle
Richelieu)](CFfacadePlacedelOpera.png "The Comédie Française (Salle Richelieu)")

Theatre traditionally has occupied a large place in Parisian culture,
and many of its most popular actors today are also stars of French
television. The oldest and most famous Paris theatre is the
[Comédie-Française](Comédie-Française "wikilink"), founded in 1680. Run
by the Government of France, it performs mostly French classics at the
Salle Richelieu in the
[Palais-Royal](Palais-Royal "wikilink").`{{Sfn|Fierro|1996|page=1173}}`{=mediawiki}
Other famous theatres include the [Odéon-Théâtre de
l\'Europe](Odéon-Théâtre_de_l'Europe "wikilink"), also a state
institution and theatrical landmark; the Théâtre Mogador; and the
[Théâtre de la
Gaîté-Montparnasse](Théâtre_de_la_Gaîté-Montparnasse "wikilink").[^175]

The music hall and [cabaret](cabaret "wikilink") are famous Paris
institutions. The _[Moulin Rouge](Moulin_Rouge "wikilink")_ opened in
1889 and became the birthplace of the [Cancan](Cancan "wikilink") dance.
It helped make famous the singers [Mistinguett](Mistinguett "wikilink")
and [Édith Piaf](Édith_Piaf "wikilink") and the painter
[Toulouse-Lautrec](Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec "wikilink"), who made
posters for the venue. In 1911, the dance hall [Olympia
Paris](Olympia_Paris "wikilink") invented the grand staircase as a
setting for its shows, competing with its great rival, the _[Folies
Bergère](Folies_Bergère "wikilink")_.

The [Casino de Paris](Casino_de_Paris "wikilink") presented many famous
French singers, including [Mistinguett](Mistinguett "wikilink"),
[Maurice Chevalier](Maurice_Chevalier "wikilink") and [Tino
Rossi](Tino_Rossi "wikilink"). Other famous Paris music halls include
_[Le Lido](Le_Lido "wikilink")_, on the Champs-Élysées, opened in 1946;
and the [Crazy Horse Saloon](<Crazy_Horse_(cabaret)> "wikilink"),
featuring strip-tease, dance, and magic, opened in 1951. A half dozen
music halls exist today in Paris, attended mostly by visitors to the
city.`{{Sfn|Fierro|1996|pages=1005–1006}}`{=mediawiki}

### Literature

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Writers in Paris}}
```

[thumb\|left\|upright=0.75\|[Victor
Hugo](Victor_Hugo "wikilink")](File:Victor_Hugo_by_Étienne_Carjat_1876_-_full.jpg "wikilink")
The first book printed in France, _Epistolae_ (\"Letters\"), by
[Gasparinus de Bergamo](Gasparinus_de_Bergamo "wikilink"), was published
in Paris in 1470 by the press established by [Johann
Heynlin](Johann_Heynlin "wikilink"). Paris has since been the centre of
the French publishing industry, the home of some of the world\'s
best-known writers and poets, and the setting for many classic works of
French literature. Paris did not become the acknowledged capital of
French literature until the 17th century, with authors such as
[Boileau](Nicolas_Boileau "wikilink"),
[Corneille](Pierre_Corneille "wikilink"), [La
Fontaine](La_Fontaine "wikilink"), [Molière](Molière "wikilink"),
[Racine](Jean_Racine "wikilink"), [Charles
Perrault](Charles_Perrault "wikilink"),[^176] several coming from the
provinces, as well as the foundation of the
`{{Lang|fr|[[Académie française]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki}.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=488}}`{=mediawiki}
In the 18th century, the literary life of Paris revolved around the
cafés and salons; it was dominated by [Voltaire](Voltaire "wikilink"),
[Jean-Jacques Rousseau](Jean-Jacques_Rousseau "wikilink"), [Pierre de
Marivaux](Pierre_de_Marivaux "wikilink"), and [Pierre
Beaumarchais](Pierre_Beaumarchais "wikilink").

During the 19th century, Paris was the home and subject for some of
France\'s greatest writers, including [Charles
Baudelaire](Charles_Baudelaire "wikilink"), [Stéphane
Mallarmé](Stéphane_Mallarmé "wikilink"), [Mérimée](Mérimée "wikilink"),
[Alfred de Musset](Alfred_de_Musset "wikilink"), [Marcel
Proust](Marcel_Proust "wikilink"), [Émile Zola](Émile_Zola "wikilink"),
[Alexandre Dumas](Alexandre_Dumas "wikilink"), [Gustave
Flaubert](Gustave_Flaubert "wikilink"), [Guy de
Maupassant](Guy_de_Maupassant "wikilink") and [Honoré de
Balzac](Honoré_de_Balzac "wikilink"). Victor Hugo\'s _[The Hunchback of
Notre-Dame](The_Hunchback_of_Notre-Dame "wikilink")_ inspired the
renovation of its setting, the [Notre-Dame de
Paris](Notre-Dame_de_Paris "wikilink").[^177] Another of Hugo\'s works,
_[Les Misérables](Les_Misérables "wikilink")_, described the social
change and political turmoil in Paris in the early 1830s.[^178] One of
the most popular of all French writers, [Jules
Verne](Jules_Verne "wikilink"), worked at the Theatre Lyrique and the
Paris stock exchange while he did research for his stories at the
National Library.[^179]

In the 20th century, the Paris literary community was dominated by
figures such as [Colette](Colette "wikilink"), [André
Gide](André_Gide "wikilink"), [François
Mauriac](François_Mauriac "wikilink"), [André
Malraux](André_Malraux "wikilink"), [Albert
Camus](Albert_Camus "wikilink"), and, after World War II, by [Simone de
Beauvoir](Simone_de_Beauvoir "wikilink") and [Jean-Paul
Sartre](Jean-Paul_Sartre "wikilink"). Between the wars, it was the home
of many important expatriate writers, including [Ernest
Hemingway](Ernest_Hemingway "wikilink"), [Samuel
Beckett](Samuel_Beckett "wikilink"), [Miguel Ángel
Asturias](Miguel_Ángel_Asturias "wikilink"), [Alejo
Carpentier](Alejo_Carpentier "wikilink"), and [Arturo Uslar
Pietri](Arturo_Uslar_Pietri "wikilink"). The winner of the 2014 [Nobel
Prize in Literature](Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "wikilink"), [Patrick
Modiano](Patrick_Modiano "wikilink"), based most of his literary work on
the depiction of the city during World War II and the
1960s--1970s.[^180]

In the 1970s, 80 percent of French-language publishing houses were found
in Paris.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=840}}`{=mediawiki} It is also a city of
small bookstores. There are about 150 bookstores in the 5th
arrondissement alone, plus another 250 book stalls along the Seine.
Small Paris bookstores are protected against competition from discount
booksellers by French law; books, even e-books, cannot be discounted
more than five percent below their publisher\'s cover price.[^181]

### Music

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Music in Paris|History of music in Paris}}
```

[thumb\|left\|upright=0.75\|[Olympia](Olympia\_(Paris) "wikilink") music
hall](File:Olympia_facade.jpg "wikilink") In the late 12th century, a
school of [polyphony](polyphony "wikilink") was established at
Notre-Dame. Among the [Trouvères](Trouvères "wikilink") of northern
France, a group of Parisian aristocrats became known for their poetry
and songs. [Troubadours](Troubadours "wikilink"), from the south of
France, were also popular. During the reign of [François
I](Francis_I_of_France "wikilink"), in the [Renaissance
era](Renaissance_music "wikilink"), the [lute](lute "wikilink") became
popular in the French court. The French royal family and courtiers
\"disported themselves in masques, ballets, allegorical dances,
recitals, and opera and comedy\", and a national musical printing house
was established.`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki} In the
[Baroque-era](Baroque_music "wikilink"), noted composers included
[Jean-Baptiste Lully](Jean-Baptiste_Lully "wikilink"), [Jean-Philippe
Rameau](Jean-Philippe_Rameau "wikilink"), and [François
Couperin](François_Couperin "wikilink").`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}
The [_Conservatoire de Musique de
Paris_](Conservatoire_de_Paris "wikilink") was founded in 1795.`{{sfn|Damschroeder|Williams|1990|p=157}}`{=mediawiki} By 1870,
Paris had become an important centre for symphony, ballet, and operatic
music.

[Romantic-era](Romantic_music "wikilink") composers include [Hector
Berlioz](Hector_Berlioz "wikilink"), [Charles
Gounod](Charles_Gounod "wikilink"), [Camille
Saint-Saëns](Camille_Saint-Saëns "wikilink"), [Léo
Delibes](Léo_Delibes "wikilink") and [Jules
Massenet](Jules_Massenet "wikilink"), among
others.`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki} [Georges
Bizet](Georges_Bizet "wikilink")\'s _[Carmen](Carmen "wikilink")_
premiered in 1875 and has become one of the most popular operas in the
classical [canon](Western_canon "wikilink").[^182][^183] Among the
[Impressionist](Impressionism_in_music "wikilink") composers who created
new works for piano, orchestra, opera, chamber music and other musical
forms, stand in particular [Claude Debussy](Claude_Debussy "wikilink"),
[Erik Satie](Erik_Satie "wikilink") and [Maurice
Ravel](Maurice_Ravel "wikilink"). Several foreign-born composers, such
as [Frédéric Chopin](Frédéric_Chopin "wikilink"), [Franz
Liszt](Franz_Liszt "wikilink"), [Jacques
Offenbach](Jacques_Offenbach "wikilink"), [Niccolò
Paganini](Niccolò_Paganini "wikilink"), and [Igor
Stravinsky](Igor_Stravinsky "wikilink"), established themselves or made
significant contributions both with their works and their influence in
Paris.

[Bal-musette](Bal-musette "wikilink") is a style of French music and
dance that first became popular in Paris in the 1870s; by 1880, Paris
had some 150 dance halls.`{{sfn|Dregni|2004|p=19}}`{=mediawiki} Patrons
danced the [bourrée](bourrée "wikilink") to the accompaniment of the
[cabrette](cabrette "wikilink") (a [bellows](bellows "wikilink")-blown
[bagpipe](bagpipe "wikilink") locally called a \"musette\") and the
vielle à roue ([hurdy-gurdy](hurdy-gurdy "wikilink")) in the cafés and
bars of the city. Parisian and Italian musicians who played the
[accordion](accordion "wikilink") adopted the style and established
themselves in Auvergnat bars,`{{sfn|Dregni|2008|p=32}}`{=mediawiki} and
Paris became a major centre for [jazz](jazz "wikilink") and still
attracts jazz musicians from all around the world to its clubs and
cafés.`{{sfn|Mroue|2006|p=260}}`{=mediawiki} Paris is the spiritual home
of [gypsy jazz](gypsy_jazz "wikilink"), and many of the Parisian jazzmen
who developed in the first half of the 20th century began by playing
Bal-musette in the city.`{{Sfn|Dregni|2008|p=32}}`{=mediawiki} [Django
Reinhardt](Django_Reinhardt "wikilink") rose to fame in Paris and
performed with violinist [Stéphane
Grappelli](Stéphane_Grappelli "wikilink") and their [Quintette du Hot
Club de France](Quintette_du_Hot_Club_de_France "wikilink") in the 1930s
and 1940s.[^184]

![The [Moulin Rouge](Moulin_Rouge "wikilink") has hosted many singers
including Parisian [Édith
Piaf](Édith_Piaf "wikilink")](Moulin_Rouge,_Paris_April_2011.jpg "The Moulin Rouge has hosted many singers including Parisian Édith Piaf")
Immediately after the war, the
[Saint-Germain-des-Prés](Saint-Germain-des-Prés "wikilink") quarter and
the nearby Saint-Michel quarter became home to many small jazz clubs,
including the Caveau des Lorientais, the Club Saint-Germain, the Rose
Rouge, the Vieux-Colombier, and the most famous, [Le
Tabou](Le_Tabou "wikilink"). They introduced Parisians to the music of
[Claude Luter](Claude_Luter "wikilink"), [Boris
Vian](Boris_Vian "wikilink"), [Sydney Bechet](Sydney_Bechet "wikilink"),
[Mezz Mezzrow](Mezz_Mezzrow "wikilink"), and [Henri
Salvador](Henri_Salvador "wikilink"). Most of the clubs closed by the
early 1960s, as musical tastes shifted toward rock and
roll.`{{Sfn|Bezbakh|2004|page=872}}`{=mediawiki}

Some of the finest [manouche](manouche "wikilink") musicians in the
world are found here playing the cafés of the city at night.[^185] Some
of the more notable jazz venues include the New Morning, Le Sunset, La
Chope des Puces and Bouquet du
Nord.`{{sfn|Mroue|2006|p=260}}`{=mediawiki}[^186] Several yearly
festivals take place in Paris, including the [Paris Jazz
Festival](Paris_Jazz_Festival "wikilink") and the rock festival [Rock en
Seine](Rock_en_Seine "wikilink").[^187] The [Orchestre de
Paris](Orchestre_de_Paris "wikilink") was established in 1967.[^188]
[Édith Piaf](Édith_Piaf "wikilink") is widely regarded as France\'s
national [chanteuse](Chanson "wikilink") and one of France\'s greatest
international stars.[^189]

Paris has a big [hip hop](French_hip_hop "wikilink") scene. This music
became popular during the 1980s.[^190]

### Cinema

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|List of films set in Paris}}
```

![Poster for the [Lumière
brothers](Auguste_and_Louis_Lumière "wikilink") 1895 film *[L'Arroseur
Arrosé](L'Arroseur_Arrosé "wikilink")*, the earliest comedy, and the
first film to portray a fictional
story.](Cinématographe_Lumière.jpg "Poster for the Lumière brothers 1895 film L'Arroseur Arrosé, the earliest comedy, and the first film to portray a fictional story.")
The movie industry was born in Paris when [Auguste and Louis
Lumière](Auguste_and_Louis_Lumière "wikilink") projected the first
motion picture for a paying audience at the Grand Café on 28 December 1895.[^191] Many of Paris\'s concert/dance halls were transformed into
cinemas when the media became popular beginning in the 1930s. Paris\'s
largest cinema room today is in the [Grand Rex](Grand_Rex "wikilink")
theatre with 2,700 seats.[^192] Big multiplex cinemas have been built
since the 1990s. UGC Ciné Cité Les Halles, with 27 screens, MK2
Bibliothèque with 20 screens, and UGC Ciné Cité Bercy with 18 screens
are among the largest.[^193]

Parisians tend to share the same movie-going trends as many of the
world\'s global cities, with cinemas primarily dominated by
Hollywood-generated film entertainment. [French
cinema](Cinema_of_France "wikilink") comes a close second, with major
directors (_réalisateurs_) such as [Claude
Lelouch](Claude_Lelouch "wikilink"), [Jean-Luc
Godard](Jean-Luc_Godard "wikilink"), and [Luc
Besson](Luc_Besson "wikilink"), and the more slapstick/popular genre
with director [Claude Zidi](Claude_Zidi "wikilink") as an example.[^194]

### `<span class="anchor" id="Cuisine">`{=html}`</span>`{=html}Restaurants and cuisine {#restaurants_and_cuisine}

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|French cuisine}}
```

![Le Zimmer, on the [Place du
Châtelet](Place_du_Châtelet "wikilink")](Le_Zimmer,_restaurant_in_Paris.jpg "Le Zimmer, on the Place du Châtelet")
Since the late 18th century, Paris has been famous for its restaurants
and _[haute cuisine](haute_cuisine "wikilink")_, food meticulously
prepared and artfully presented. La Taverne Anglaise, opened in 1786 in
the arcades of the [Palais-Royal](Palais-Royal "wikilink") by [Antoine
Beauvilliers](Antoine_Beauvilliers "wikilink"); it became a model for
future luxury Paris restaurants. The restaurant [Le Grand
Véfour](Le_Grand_Véfour "wikilink") in the Palais-Royal dates from the
same period.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|pp=1136–1138}}`{=mediawiki} The famous
Paris restaurants of the 19th century, including the Café de Paris, the
[Rocher de Cancale](Au_Rocher_de_Cancale "wikilink"), the [Café
Anglais](Café_Anglais "wikilink"), [Maison
Dorée](<Maison_dorée_(Paris)> "wikilink") and the Café Riche, were mostly
located near the theatres on the [Boulevard des
Italiens](Boulevard_des_Italiens "wikilink"). Several of the best-known
restaurants in Paris today appeared during the *[Belle
Époque](Belle*Époque "wikilink")\_, including
[Maxim\'s](Maxim's_Paris "wikilink") on Rue Royale,
[Ledoyen](Ledoyen "wikilink") in the gardens of the
[Champs-Élysées](Champs-Élysées "wikilink"), and the [Tour
d\'Argent](Tour_d'Argent "wikilink") on the Quai de la
Tournelle.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=1137}}`{=mediawiki}

Today, owing to Paris\'s cosmopolitan population, every French regional
cuisine and almost every national cuisine in the world can be found
there; the city has more than 9,000
restaurants.`{{sfn|Dominé|2014}}`{=mediawiki} The [Michelin
Guide](Michelin_Guide "wikilink") has been a standard guide to French
restaurants since 1900, awarding its highest award, three stars, to the
best restaurants in France. In 2018, of the 27 Michelin three-star
restaurants in France, ten are located in Paris. These include both
restaurants that serve classical French cuisine, such as
[L\'Ambroisie](L'Ambroisie "wikilink"), and those that serve
non-traditional menus, such as [L\'Astrance](L'Astrance "wikilink"),
which combines French and Asian cuisines. Several of France\'s most
famous chefs, including [Pierre Gagnaire](Pierre_Gagnaire "wikilink"),
[Alain Ducasse](Alain_Ducasse "wikilink"), [Yannick
Alléno](Yannick_Alléno "wikilink"), and [Alain
Passard](Alain_Passard "wikilink"), have three-star restaurants in
Paris.[^195][^196]

![`{{lang|fr|[[Les Deux Magots]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki} café on
Boulevard
Saint-Germain](Lesdeuxmagots.jpg " café on Boulevard Saint-Germain")
Paris has several other kinds of traditional eating places. The
[café](Coffeehouse "wikilink") arrived in Paris in the 17th century, and
by the 18th century, Parisian cafés were centres of the city\'s
political and cultural life. The [Café Procope](Café_Procope "wikilink")
on the Left Bank dates from this period. In the 20th century, the cafés
of the Left Bank, especially [Café de la
Rotonde](Café_de_la_Rotonde "wikilink") and [Le Dôme
Café](Le_Dôme_Café "wikilink") in Montparnasse and [Café de
Flore](Café_de_Flore "wikilink") and
`{{lang|fr|[[Les Deux Magots]]|italic=no}}`{=mediawiki} on Boulevard
Saint Germain, all still in business, were important meeting places for
painters, writers and
philosophers.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=1137}}`{=mediawiki} A
[bistro](bistro "wikilink") is a type of eating place loosely defined as
a neighbourhood restaurant with a modest decor and prices and a regular
clientele, and a congenial atmosphere. Real bistros are increasingly
rare in Paris, due to rising costs, competition, and different eating
habits of Parisian diners.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=715}}`{=mediawiki} A
[brasserie](brasserie "wikilink") originally was a tavern located next
to a brewery, which served beer and food at any hour. Beginning with the
[Paris Exposition of 1867](Paris_Exposition_of_1867 "wikilink"), it
became a popular kind of restaurant which featured beer and other
beverages served by young women in the national costume associated with
the beverage. Now, brasseries, like cafés, serve food and drinks
throughout the day.`{{sfn|Fierro|1996|p=773}}`{=mediawiki}

### Photography

The inventor [Nicéphore Niépce](Nicéphore_Niépce "wikilink") produced
the first permanent photograph on a polished pewter plate in Paris in

1825. In 1839, [Louis Daguerre](Louis_Daguerre "wikilink") patented the
      [Daguerreotype](Daguerreotype "wikilink"), which became the most common
      form of photography until the 1860s.`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}
      The work of [Étienne-Jules Marey](Étienne-Jules_Marey "wikilink") in the
      1880s contributed considerably to the development of modern photography.
      Photography came to occupy a central role in Parisian Surrealist
      activity, in the works of [Man Ray](Man_Ray "wikilink") and [Maurice
      Tabard](Maurice_Tabard "wikilink").[^201]`{{sfn|Hazan|2011|p=362}}`{=mediawiki}
      Numerous photographers achieved renown for their photography of Paris,
      including [Eugène Atget](Eugène_Atget "wikilink"), noted for his
      depictions of street scenes; [Robert
      Doisneau](Robert_Doisneau "wikilink"), noted for his playful pictures of
      people and market scenes (among which _Le baiser de l\'hôtel de ville_
      has become iconic of the romantic vision of Paris); [Marcel
      Bovis](Marcel_Bovis "wikilink"), noted for his night scenes;
      [Jacques-Henri Lartigue](Jacques-Henri_Lartigue "wikilink"); and [Henri
      Cartier-Bresson](Henri_Cartier-Bresson "wikilink").`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}
      [Poster art](Poster_art "wikilink") also became an important art form in
      Paris in the late nineteenth century, through the work of [Henri de
      Toulouse-Lautrec](Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec "wikilink"), [Jules
      Chéret](Jules_Chéret "wikilink"), [Eugène
      Grasset](Eugène_Grasset "wikilink"), [Adolphe
      Willette](Adolphe_Willette "wikilink"), [Pierre
      Bonnard](Pierre_Bonnard "wikilink"), [Georges de
      Feure](Georges_de_Feure "wikilink"), [Henri-Gabriel
      Ibels](Henri-Gabriel_Ibels "wikilink"), [Paul
      Gavarni](Paul_Gavarni "wikilink") and [Alphonse
      Mucha](Alphonse_Mucha "wikilink").`{{sfn|Michelin|2011}}`{=mediawiki}

### Holidays and festivals {#holidays_and_festivals}

[Bastille Day](Bastille_Day "wikilink"), a celebration of the storming
of the Bastille in 1789, the biggest festival in the city, is a military
parade taking place every year on 14 July on the
[Champs-Élysées](Champs-Élysées "wikilink"), from the [Arc de
Triomphe](Arc_de_Triomphe "wikilink") to [Place de la
Concorde](Place_de_la_Concorde "wikilink"). It includes a
[flypast](flypast "wikilink") over the Champs Élysées by the [Patrouille
de France](Patrouille_de_France "wikilink"), a parade of military units
and equipment, and a display of fireworks in the evening, the most
spectacular being the one at the Eiffel
Tower.`{{sfn|BlackmoreMcConnachie|2004|p=204}}`{=mediawiki}

Other yearly festivals include [Paris-Plages](Paris-Plages "wikilink"),
a festive summertime event when the Right Bank of the Seine is converted
into a temporary
beach;`{{sfn|BlackmoreMcConnachie|2004|p=204}}`{=mediawiki} [Journées du
Patrimoine](European_Heritage_Days "wikilink"), [Fête de la
Musique](Fête_de_la_Musique "wikilink"), Techno Parade, [Nuit
Blanche](Nuit_Blanche "wikilink"), Cinéma au clair de lune, Printemps
des rues, Festival d\'automne, and Fête des jardins. The [Carnaval de
Paris](Paris_Carnival "wikilink"), one of the oldest festivals in Paris,
dates back to the Middle Ages.

![[Sainte-Geneviève
Library](Sainte-Geneviève_Library "wikilink")](Salle_de_lecture_Bibliotheque_Sainte-Genevieve_n01.jpg "Sainte-Geneviève Library")

The [Bibliothèque Forney](Bibliothèque_Forney "wikilink"), in the Marais
district, is dedicated to the decorative arts; the Arsenal Library
occupies a former military building, and has a large collection on
French literature; and the [Bibliothèque historique de la ville de
Paris](Bibliothèque_historique_de_la_ville_de_Paris "wikilink"), also in
Le Marais, contains the Paris historical research service. The
[Sainte-Geneviève Library](Sainte-Geneviève_Library "wikilink"),
designed by [Henri Labrouste](Henri_Labrouste "wikilink") and built in
the mid-1800s, contains a rare book and manuscript division.[^212]
[Bibliothèque Mazarine](Bibliothèque_Mazarine "wikilink") is the oldest
public library in France. The [Médiathèque Musicale
Mahler](Médiathèque_Musicale_Mahler "wikilink") opened in 1986 and
contains collections related to music. The François Mitterrand Library
(nicknamed _Très Grande Bibliothèque_) was completed in 1994 to a design
of [Dominique Perrault](Dominique_Perrault "wikilink") and contains four
glass towers.[^213]

There are several academic libraries and archives in Paris. The
[Sorbonne Library](Bibliothèque_de_la_Sorbonne "wikilink") is the
largest university library in Paris. In addition to the
[Sorbonne](<Sorbonne_(building)> "wikilink") location, there are branches
in Malesherbes, Clignancourt-Championnet, Michelet-[Institut d\'Art et
d\'Archéologie](Institut_d'Art_et_d'Archéologie "wikilink"),
Serpente-Maison de la Recherche, and Institut des Etudes
Ibériques.[^214] Other academic libraries include Interuniversity
Pharmaceutical Library, Leonardo da Vinci University Library, Paris
School of Mines Library, and the René Descartes University
Library.[^215]

## Infrastructure

### Transport

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Transport in Paris}}
```

![The [Gare du Nord](Gare_du_Nord "wikilink") railway station is the
busiest in
Europe.](Eurostar_Paris_Gare_du_Nord_Station,_8_October_2011.jpg "The Gare du Nord railway station is the busiest in Europe.")
Paris is a major rail, highway, and air transport hub. The
[Île-de-France Mobilités](Île-de-France_Mobilités "wikilink") (IDFM)
oversees the transit network in the region.[^232] The syndicate
coordinates public transport. The [RATP](RATP_Group "wikilink") operates
347 [bus](<Bus_(RATP)> "wikilink") lines, the
[Métro](Paris_Métro "wikilink"), eight tramway lines, and sections of
the RER. The [SNCF](SNCF "wikilink") operates suburban rails, one
tramway line, and other sections of the RER. The
[Optile](Optile "wikilink") consortium of private operators manages
1,176 bus lines.[^233]

Paris has one of the most
[sustainable](Sustainable_transport "wikilink") transportation systems
in the world[^234] and is one of only two cities that received the
[Sustainable Transport Award](Sustainable_Transport_Award "wikilink")
twice, in 2008 and 2023.[^235] In 2022--2023, 53.3% of trips in Paris
were made on foot, 30% on public transport, 11.2% on bicycles and 4.3%
on cars.[^236][^237] Paris has banned the most polluting automobiles
from key districts[^238][^239] and is doubling bike lanes. The concept
of the [15-minute city](15-minute_city "wikilink") was created by
[Carlos Moreno](<Carlos_Moreno_(urbanist)> "wikilink"), a professor from
Paris, and began to be implemented by its mayor [Anne
Hidalgo](Anne_Hidalgo "wikilink").[^240]

#### Walking

Paris is a highly [walkable](walkable "wikilink") city and tries to
increase its mode share beyond[^241][^242] 53% of all trips in 2024.[^243] The number of trips made by foot in the Grand Paris
metropolis increased by 50% from 2000 to 2018.[^244]

A central hub of the national rail network, Paris\'s six major railway
stations ([Gare du Nord](Gare_du_Nord "wikilink"), [Gare de
l\'Est](Gare_de_l'Est "wikilink"), [Gare de

[high-speed rail](high-speed_rail "wikilink") lines ([TGV
inOui](TGV "wikilink"), [TGV Lyria](TGV_Lyria "wikilink"), [TGV
Ouigo](Ouigo "wikilink"), [Eurostar](Eurostar "wikilink"), [Intercity

([Intercités](Intercités "wikilink"), [Ouigo Train

nuit](Intercités_de_nuit "wikilink"), [European
Sleeper](European_Sleeper "wikilink"),
[TER](Transport_express_régional "wikilink")), and the suburban rails
([Transilien](Transilien "wikilink")). The Transilien is the [commuter
rail](commuter_rail "wikilink") network serving [Paris
region](Île-de-France "wikilink"), through 9 lines, 392 stations and
`{{cvt|1,294|km|mi|1}}`{=mediawiki} of rails.

Since the inauguration of its first line in 1900, Paris\'s Métro network
has grown to become the city\'s most widely used local transport system.
In 2015, it carried about 5.23 million passengers daily.[^245] There are
16 lines, 321 stations (405 stops) and
`{{cvt|245.6|km|mi|1}}`{=mediawiki} of rails. Superimposed on this is a
[regional express network](Réseau_Express_Régional "wikilink"), the RER,
whose five lines, 257 stops, and `{{cvt|602|km|mi|0}}`{=mediawiki} of
rails connect Paris to more distant parts of the urban area. With over
1.4 million passengers per day, [RER A](RER_A "wikilink") is the busiest
metro line in Europe. The [Paris region](Île-de-France "wikilink") is
served by [a light rail network, the
tramway](Tramways_in_Île-de-France "wikilink"). Opened in 1992, fifteen
lines are now operational, spanning
`{{convert|196.6|km|mi}}`{=mediawiki} with 283 stations.

#### Air

Paris is served by three commercial international airports: [Charles de
Gaulle Airport](Charles_de_Gaulle_Airport "wikilink"), the
[third-busiest airport in
Europe](List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_Europe "wikilink")
`{{as of|2018|lc=y}}`{=mediawiki},[^248][^249] [Orly
Airport](Orly_Airport "wikilink") and [Beauvais--Tillé
Airport](Beauvais–Tillé_Airport "wikilink"). In 2019, these three
airports recorded traffic of 112 million passengers,[^250] making them
the [5th busiest airport system in the

There is also one [general aviation](general_aviation "wikilink")
airport, [Paris--Le Bourget

Parisian airport and closest to the city centre, which is now used only

international
traffic](List_of_busiest_airports_by_international_passenger_traffic "wikilink")
and it is the hub for the nation\'s [flag
carrier](flag_carrier "wikilink"), [Air
France](Air_France "wikilink").`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|pp=278–283}}`{=mediawiki}
Beauvais-Tillé Airport, located `{{cvt|69|km|abbr=off}}`{=mediawiki}
north of Paris\'s city centre, is used by charter airlines and [low-cost
carriers](low-cost_carriers "wikilink").

Paris is the most important hub of France\'s

The Paris region is the most active water transport area in France. Most
of the cargo is handled by the [Ports of

### Electricity

```{=mediawiki}
{{See also|Electricity sector in France}}
```

In 2012, around 50% of electricity generated in the
[Île-de-France](Île-de-France "wikilink") came from cogeneration energy
plants. Other energy sources included thermal power (35%), waste
incineration (9% -- with cogeneration plants, these provide the city
with heat as well), methane gas (5%), hydraulics (1%), and solar power
(0.1%).[^252] A quarter of the city\'s [district
heating](district_heating "wikilink") is to come from a plant in
[Saint-Ouen-sur-Seine](Saint-Ouen-sur-Seine "wikilink"), burning a
50/50-mix of coal and [wood pellets](wood_pellets "wikilink").[^253]

### Water and sanitation {#water_and_sanitation}

![The Seine, the [Île de la Cité](Île_de_la_Cité "wikilink") and a
[Bateau
Mouche](Bateau_Mouche "wikilink")](Ile_de_la_Cite_from_Pont_de_la_Tournelle.jpg "The Seine, the Île de la Cité and a Bateau Mouche")
Paris in its early history had only the rivers Seine and
[Bièvre](<Bièvre_(river)> "wikilink") for water. From 1809, the [Canal de
l\'Ourcq](Canal_de_l'Ourcq "wikilink") provided Paris with water from
less-polluted rivers to the north-east of the capital.[^254] From 1857,
the civil engineer [Eugène Belgrand](Eugène_Belgrand "wikilink"), under
[Napoleon III](Napoleon_III "wikilink"), oversaw the construction of a
series of new aqueducts that brought water from locations all around the
city to several reservoirs.`{{sfn|Burchell|1971|p=93}}`{=mediawiki}

From then on, the new reservoir system became Paris\'s principal source
of drinking water. The remains of the old system, pumped into lower
levels of the same reservoirs, were from then on used for the cleaning
of Paris\'s streets. This system is still a major part of Paris\'s
water-supply network. Today, Paris has more than
`{{cvt|2400|km|0}}`{=mediawiki} of underground sewers.[^255]

[Air pollution](Air_pollution "wikilink") in Paris, from the point of
view of [particulate matter](Particulates "wikilink") (PM10), is the
highest in France with 38 μg/m^3^.[^256] Paris has one of the highest
levels of nitrogen dioxide pollution in the EU.[^257]

### Parks and gardens {#parks_and_gardens}

Paris has more than 421 municipal parks and gardens, covering more than
3,000 hectares and containing more than 250,000
trees.`{{Sfn|Jarrassé|2007|p=6}}`{=mediawiki} Two of Paris\'s oldest and
most famous gardens are the [Tuileries
Garden](Tuileries_Garden "wikilink"), created in 1564 for the [Tuileries

1672,`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=125}}`{=mediawiki} and the

Palace](Luxembourg_Palace "wikilink"), built for [Marie de\'
Medici](Marie_de'_Medici "wikilink") in 1612, which today houses the
[Senate](<Senate_(France)> "wikilink").`{{sfn|Lawrence|Gondrand|2010|p=208}}`{=mediawiki}
The _[Jardin des plantes](Jardin_des_plantes "wikilink")_ was the first
botanical garden in Paris, created in 1626.[^258]

Between 1853 and 1870, Emperor [Napoleon III](Napoleon_III "wikilink")
and the city\'s first director of parks and gardens, [Jean-Charles
Adolphe Alphand](Jean-Charles_Adolphe_Alphand "wikilink"), created the

Montsouris](Parc_Montsouris "wikilink") and [Parc des
Buttes-Chaumont](Parc_des_Buttes-Chaumont "wikilink"), located at the
four compass points around Paris, as well as many smaller parks, squares
and gardens in the Paris\'s
quarters.`{{Sfn|Jarrassé|2007|pp=122–161}}`{=mediawiki} Since 1977, the
city has created 166 new parks, most notably the [Parc de la
Villette](Parc_de_la_Villette "wikilink") (1987), [Parc André
Citroën](Parc_André_Citroën "wikilink") (1992), [Parc de
Bercy](Parc_de_Bercy "wikilink") (1997) and [Parc
Clichy-Batignolles](Parc_Clichy-Batignolles_–_Martin-Luther-King "wikilink")
(2007).`{{Sfn|Jarrassé|2007|pp=242–256}}`{=mediawiki} One of the newest
parks, the [Promenade des Berges de la
Seine](Promenade_des_Berges_de_la_Seine "wikilink") (2013), has floating
gardens.

### Cemeteries

![The [Paris Catacombs](Catacombs_of_Paris "wikilink") hold the remains
of approximately 6 million
people.](Catacombes_De_Paris.jpg "The Paris Catacombs hold the remains of approximately 6 million people.")
During the Roman era, Paris\'s main cemetery was located on the
outskirts of the [left bank](Rive_Gauche "wikilink") settlement. This
changed with the rise of Catholic Christianity, where almost every
inner-city church had adjoining burial grounds for use by their
parishes. With Paris\'s growth, many of these, particularly the city\'s
largest cemetery, the [Holy Innocents\'
Cemetery](Holy_Innocents'_Cemetery "wikilink"), were filled to
overflowing. When inner-city burials were condemned from 1786, the
contents of all Paris\'s parish cemeteries were transferred to a
renovated section of [Paris\'s stone mines](Mines_of_Paris "wikilink"),
today [place Denfert-Rochereau](place_Denfert-Rochereau "wikilink") in
the 14th
arrondissement.`{{sfn|Whaley|2012|p=101}}`{=mediawiki}`{{sfn|Broadwell|2007|p=92}}`{=mediawiki}

After a tentative creation of several smaller suburban cemeteries, the
Prefect Nicholas Frochot under [Napoleon](Napoleon "wikilink") Bonaparte
provided a more definitive solution in the creation of three massive
Parisian cemeteries outside the city
limits.`{{sfn|Ayers|2004|p=271}}`{=mediawiki} Open from 1804, these were
the cemeteries of [Père Lachaise](Père_Lachaise_Cemetery "wikilink"),
[Montmartre](Montmartre_Cemetery "wikilink"),
[Montparnasse](Montparnasse_Cemetery "wikilink"), and later
[Passy](Passy_Cemetery "wikilink"). New suburban cemeteries were created
in the early 20th century: The largest of these are the [Cimetière
parisien de Saint-Ouen](Saint-Ouen_Cemetery "wikilink"), the [Cimetière
parisien de Pantin](Cimetière_parisien_de_Pantin "wikilink"), also known
as Cimetière parisien de
[Pantin](Pantin "wikilink")-[Bobigny](Bobigny "wikilink"), the Cimetière
parisien d\'[Ivry](Ivry-sur-Seine "wikilink"), and the Cimetière
parisien de [Bagneux](Bagneux,_Hauts-de-Seine "wikilink").[^259] Famous
people buried in Parisian cemeteries include [Oscar
Wilde](Oscar_Wilde "wikilink"), [Frédéric
Chopin](Frédéric_Chopin "wikilink"), [Jim
Morrison](Jim_Morrison "wikilink"), [Édith Piaf](Édith_Piaf "wikilink")
and [Serge Gainsbourg](Serge_Gainsbourg "wikilink").[^260]

### Education

```{=mediawiki}
{{Main|Education in Paris}}
```

[upright=0.75\|thumb\|The [Sorbonne
University](Sorbonne_University "wikilink")](File:Chapelle_Sainte-Ursule_de_la_Sorbonne,_Paris_001.jpg "wikilink")
In 2009, around 40 percent of Parisians held a
_[licence](<Licence_(France)> "wikilink")\_-level diploma or higher, the
highest proportion in France.[^261] 13 percent have no diploma, the
third-lowest percentage in France. Education in Paris and the
Île-de-France region employs approximately 330,000 people, 170,000 of
whom are teachers and professors, teaching approximately 2.9 million
students in around 9,000 primary, secondary, and higher education
schools and institutions.[^262]

The [University of Paris](University_of_Paris "wikilink"), founded in
the 12th century, is often called the
[Sorbonne](University_of_Paris "wikilink") after one of its original
medieval colleges. In 1970, it was broken up into [thirteen autonomous
universities](List_of_universities_and_institutions_in_the_Paris_region "wikilink")
following the [student demonstrations in 1968](May_68 "wikilink"). Most
of the campuses today are in the [Latin
Quarter](Latin_Quarter,_Paris "wikilink"), where the old university was
located, while others are scattered around the city and the
suburbs.`{{sfn|Combeau|2013|pages=213–214}}`{=mediawiki}

The Paris region hosts France\'s highest concentration of the *[grandes
écoles](grandes*écoles "wikilink")_ -- 55 specialised centres of higher
education outside or inside the public university structure. The
prestigious public universities are usually considered _[grands
établissements](grands_établissements "wikilink")_. Most of the \_grandes
écoles_ were relocated to the suburbs of Paris in the 1960s and 1970s,
in new campuses much larger than the old campuses within the crowded
City of Paris. The [École Normale
Supérieure](École_Normale_Supérieure "wikilink"), [PSL
University](Paris_Sciences_et_Lettres_University "wikilink") has
remained on rue d\'Ulm in the [5th
arrondissement](5th_arrondissement_of_Paris "wikilink").[^263]

Paris is the home of prestigious universities in science and technology
([Conservatoire national des arts et
métiers](Conservatoire_national_des_arts_et_métiers "wikilink"),
[Polytechnic Institute of
Paris](Polytechnic_Institute_of_Paris "wikilink"), [Paris Cité
University](Paris_Cité_University "wikilink"), [Paris-Saclay
University](Paris-Saclay_University "wikilink"), [Sorbonne
University](Sorbonne_University "wikilink")), political science
([Sciences Po](Sciences_Po "wikilink")),[^264] management ([HEC
Paris](HEC_Paris "wikilink"), [ESSEC Business
School](ESSEC_Business_School "wikilink"), [ESCP Business
School](ESCP_Business_School "wikilink"),
[INSEAD](INSEAD "wikilink"))[^265] as well as multidisciplinary
universities ([Paris Sciences et Lettres
University](Paris_Sciences_et_Lettres_University "wikilink")).[^266]

### Healthcare

[thumb\|upright=0.9\|The [Hôtel-Dieu de
Paris](Hôtel-Dieu_de_Paris "wikilink") is the oldest hospital in
Paris.](File:Hôtel-Dieu_de_Paris,_February_7,_2013.jpg "wikilink")
Health care and emergency medical service in the City of Paris and its
suburbs are provided by the [Assistance publique -- Hôpitaux de
Paris](Assistance_publique_–_Hôpitaux_de_Paris "wikilink") (AP-HP), a
public hospital system that employs more than 90,000 people, including
practitioners, support personnel, and administrators, in 44
hospitals.[^267] It is the largest hospital system in Europe. The
hospitals receive more than 5.8 million annual patient visits.[^268]

One of the most notable hospitals is the
[Hôtel-Dieu](Hôtel-Dieu_de_Paris "wikilink"), founded in 651, the oldest
hospital in Paris and the oldest worldwide still operating,[^269]
although the current building is the product of a reconstruction of

1877. Other hospitals include [Pitié-Salpêtrière
      Hospital](Pitié-Salpêtrière_Hospital "wikilink"), one of the largest in
      Europe, [Hôpital Cochin](Hôpital_Cochin "wikilink"), [Bichat--Claude
      Bernard Hospital](Bichat–Claude_Bernard_Hospital "wikilink"), [Hôpital
      Européen
      Georges-Pompidou](Hôpital_Européen_Georges-Pompidou "wikilink"),
      [Bicêtre Hospital](Bicêtre_Hospital "wikilink"), [Beaujon
      Hospital](Beaujon_Hospital "wikilink"), the [Curie
      Institute](<Curie_Institute_(Paris)> "wikilink"), [Lariboisière
      Hospital](Lariboisière_Hospital "wikilink"), [Necker--Enfants Malades
      Hospital](Necker–Enfants_Malades_Hospital "wikilink"), [Hôpital
      Saint-Louis](Hôpital_Saint-Louis "wikilink"), [Hôpital de la
      Charité](Hôpital_de_la_Charité "wikilink") and the [American Hospital of
      Paris](American_Hospital_of_Paris "wikilink").

## International relations {#international_relations}

### International organisations {#international_organisations}

The [United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural
Organization](UNESCO "wikilink") (UNESCO) has had its headquarters in
Paris since November 1958. Paris is also the home of the [Organisation
for Economic Co-operation and
Development](Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development "wikilink")
(OECD).[^270] Paris hosts the headquarters of the [European Space
Agency](European_Space_Agency "wikilink"), the [International Energy
Agency](International_Energy_Agency "wikilink"), [European Securities
and Markets
Authority](European_Securities_and_Markets_Authority "wikilink") and the
[European Banking Authority](European_Banking_Authority "wikilink").
